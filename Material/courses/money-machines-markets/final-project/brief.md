# Final Project — A Personal Thesis

## The prompt

Write a 1,500-word personal thesis on the convergence of money, machines, and markets. By the end, the reader should be able to answer three questions:

1. **What do you actually believe** about where this is going over the next ten years?
2. **What are you uncertain about**, specifically?
3. **What would change your mind**?

This is the only thing the entire course was building toward. The eight modules existed so that you would have the vocabulary, the history, and the trade-offs in your head when you sat down to write this.

## Constraints

- **1,500 words.** (±150.) Anything shorter and you haven't said enough. Anything longer and you're hiding behind volume.
- **A single, defensible thesis statement in the first paragraph.** A reader should be able to summarize your position in one sentence.
- **Engage explicitly with vocabulary from at least four different modules.** Stock-to-flow, monetary premium, proof-of-work, scaling laws, agentic systems, regulatory capture — whatever fits. The point is that your thesis should *use* the course's machinery, not float free of it.
- **Steel-man your strongest opponent.** Identify the smartest person who would disagree with you, summarize their position fairly, and respond to it. Not the strawman version.
- **Name three specific things that would change your mind.** Not vague things like "if Bitcoin failed." Specific things like "if the global stablecoin float exceeds physical dollar circulation by 2030" or "if frontier model capability plateaus for 24 consecutive months without a new architecture." Falsifiable, observable, dated.

## What I am NOT looking for

- A book report on the modules.
- A prediction of crypto prices.
- A manifesto.
- A both-sides essay that refuses to take a position.
- AI-generated prose dressed up as your own voice.

## What I AM looking for

- A point of view you would defend in a room of skeptics.
- Honest signals about your own confidence — where you are sure, where you are guessing, where you are afraid you might be wrong.
- Evidence that you have been changed by the course. Not necessarily converted to anything. *Changed* — you noticed something you wouldn't have noticed at the start.

## Format

- Markdown.
- Tag the version of the course you took at the top: `course: money-machines-markets@v1`.
- Disclose any AI tool use at the bottom, per the AI Use Policy.

## Review

Your thesis will be reviewed by:

1. **The instructor**, against the Written Thesis Rubric (`rubrics/written-thesis.json`).
2. **One peer**, who will read it and write a 200-word response (you will also be assigned one peer thesis to review). The peer review is graded on the discussion-quality rubric.
3. **Optionally**, a voice defense — a 15-minute conversation with an AI skeptic, similar to the module voice sessions but harder. This is optional for everyone except students who want to put their thesis up on the public course wall.

## Why this exists

Most courses end with a test. A test asks: did you absorb what I taught? This course ends with a thesis because the question I actually care about is different: *do you now have a view that wasn't possible before you took this course?*

If the answer is no — if you walked in with a position and walked out with the same position — then either I failed or you didn't engage. The point of the eight modules was to put enough pressure on every comfortable answer that the answer you arrive at on the other side is *yours*, in a way it wasn't when you started.

That's the only thing worth leaving with.
