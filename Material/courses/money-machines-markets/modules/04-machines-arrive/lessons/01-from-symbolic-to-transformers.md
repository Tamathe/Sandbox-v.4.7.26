## Reading

Most people who know nothing about the history of AI imagine a smooth curve from "computers used to be dumb" to "now they can write your emails." That curve is wrong in both directions. The actual history is roughly seventy years of failure punctuated by a small number of architectural shifts, each of which the field's leading researchers said was impossible until it worked. Knowing which shifts happened and why makes the rest of this module make sense. Skip it and you will keep mistaking the current moment for either a magic trick or an inevitability. It is neither.

The first era, roughly 1956 through the late 1980s, is **symbolic AI**, sometimes called Good Old-Fashioned AI or GOFAI. The idea: intelligence is symbol manipulation. If you wrote down enough rules in formal logic, the system would *reason*. Expert systems, theorem provers, planning algorithms — all of them assumed that human knowledge could be encoded as facts and rules, and that intelligence would emerge from manipulating those rules well. This approach produced some genuinely impressive narrow systems (chess engines, medical diagnosis tools) and one enormous failure: it could not handle perception, language, or any task where the rules weren't already known. The bottleneck was that the world is not made of clean symbols, and the moment you tried to write down rules for "how to recognize a cat in a photograph" or "what does this English sentence mean," the rules exploded into millions of edge cases nobody could enumerate. Symbolic AI hit a wall by the late 1980s and went into what is now called the **first AI winter**.

The second era, roughly 1990 through 2011, is **statistical machine learning**. The shift was philosophical: stop trying to write rules, start showing the system examples and letting it learn statistical patterns. Support vector machines, decision trees, naive Bayes, logistic regression — none of them tried to "understand" anything. They learned correlations from data and made predictions. This approach quietly powered most of the things people now think of as "old internet AI": spam filters, recommendation systems, search ranking. It worked, and it worked well enough that nobody outside the field talked about AI for a decade. The bottleneck was that these systems needed *features* — humans had to decide what to measure about each input — and feature engineering was slow, fragile, and didn't generalize.

The third era, roughly 2012 through 2017, is **deep learning**. The shift was that neural networks, which had existed in some form since the 1950s, suddenly started working when you made them deep (many layers) and trained them on large datasets with GPUs. The watershed moment was AlexNet in 2012, which won an image recognition contest by such a wide margin that everyone in the field had to switch what they were working on. The breakthrough was that a deep network *learned its own features* — you didn't have to tell it what to look for in an image; it figured out edges, then textures, then shapes, then objects, by itself. For five years, deep learning ate every benchmark in vision, speech, and translation. The bottleneck was that the architectures were still task-specific. A network trained on images couldn't read text. A network trained on translation couldn't answer questions. Each problem needed its own custom design.

The fourth era, 2017 to now, is the **transformer era**, and this is the one that broke the pattern. In 2017, a Google research team published a paper called "Attention Is All You Need" describing an architecture that could process sequences (text, but also images, audio, code) using a mechanism called self-attention. The transformer turned out to have an unexpected property: it scaled. If you made it bigger and trained it on more data, it just kept getting better, and the improvements transferred across tasks. A single architecture, trained on enough text, learned to translate, summarize, answer questions, write code, and explain jokes — without anyone designing those abilities into it specifically. This is the central observation that defines the modern era and that everything since GPT-3 (2020) has been amplifying: **a sufficiently large neural network trained on a sufficiently large pile of human-generated text learns to do an enormous range of things that nobody explicitly programmed it to do.**

This observation has a name. It's called the **scaling hypothesis**, and it deserves to be stated precisely because the loose version is what makes intelligent people roll their eyes. The precise version: across multiple orders of magnitude of model size, data size, and compute budget, the loss of these models has fallen along smooth power laws, and many capabilities have emerged at predictable thresholds. This is empirical, not philosophical. It is not the claim that "more compute will get us to AGI," which is a guess. It is the claim that "for the regime we've explored so far, capabilities have continued to scale predictably with resources." Whether the curve continues, bends, or breaks at the next order of magnitude is exactly what the frontier labs are spending billions of dollars to find out, and is exactly the question whose answer will determine whether 2026's pace continues or stalls.

The honest position on the scaling hypothesis is that it is the most important empirical observation in the field and also that it might be about to stop holding. Both of those things can be true at the same time. The maximalist case ("scale alone gets us to general intelligence") rests on extrapolating a curve we have only seen the lower end of. The dismissive case ("it's just statistics, it isn't really intelligent") rests on a definition of intelligence that no one can pin down without circularity. Neither is honest. The honest position is that we are running an experiment on a curve we don't understand and finding out what happens, which is exactly what makes this an interesting moment to live through and a dangerous one to be confident about.

## Concrete example

In 2020, OpenAI released GPT-3, a transformer with 175 billion parameters trained on roughly 500 billion tokens of internet text. Nobody had explicitly trained it to do arithmetic, to write Python code, to translate French to Mandarin, or to explain a joke. It could do all of those things, badly but recognizably, simply because the training data contained examples of all of them and the architecture was big enough to generalize. The interesting part was not any single capability — it was that the *list* of capabilities was longer than the list of things the researchers had been measuring, and longer than they could fully enumerate. People are still finding things GPT-3 could do that nobody noticed at the time. Every model since (GPT-4, GPT-5, Claude 3 / 4 / 4.5, Gemini, Grok) has extended this pattern: capabilities show up that no one specifically engineered, and the engineering work shifts from "how do we build the capability" to "how do we measure what the model already knows." That shift is the most important methodological change in the field's history, and it is downstream of one architecture working better than expected.

## Uncomfortable question

If the most important empirical fact in modern AI is that capabilities emerge from scale in ways researchers cannot predict in advance, then *no one currently working on these systems can confidently tell you what the next model will and will not be able to do.* Including the people building it. How does that change your relationship to claims that frontier AI is "still just autocomplete" or "obviously about to take everyone's job"? Both of those framings are confidence claims about an experiment whose results haven't come in yet. Which one are you defaulting to, and what would have to happen for you to update?
