## Reading

If you only remember one thing from this lesson, remember this: **the cost of training a frontier model has been doubling roughly every six to ten months for years, and this has reshaped the entire competitive landscape of AI in ways that have very little to do with what the models can do and a lot to do with who can afford to build them.** The capability curve gets the headlines. The cost curve gets the industry.

A frontier model is built in three (sometimes four) distinct training stages, and you need to know what each one does, because they change the model in different ways and each costs a different amount.

**Pretraining** is the big one. The model starts as a network of randomly-initialized weights — total noise — and is shown an enormous corpus of text (and sometimes code, images, and audio). The training objective is simple: given a sequence of tokens, predict the next token. The model adjusts its weights billions of times over weeks or months of running on tens of thousands of GPUs. By the end, the random noise has become a function that has internalized a representation of the patterns in the corpus. Pretraining is where almost all of the cost is. For a frontier model in 2026, pretraining costs are in the high hundreds of millions to low billions of dollars. The 2020 GPT-3 pretraining cost about $5 million in compute. The 2023 GPT-4 pretraining cost is rumored to have been around $100 million. The 2025–2026 frontier runs are estimated north of $500 million each, with some labs spending into the billions for their flagships. The doubling has not stopped.

A pretrained model is not yet useful in the way you experience it as a chatbot. It will autocomplete text in a vague, internet-flavored way. It has no concept of "questions" or "answers." It will continue any prompt the way the internet continues things, which means it will sometimes produce useful answers and sometimes drift into spam, ranting, or nonsense. To get from pretrained to useful, you need the second stage.

**Fine-tuning** (sometimes called supervised fine-tuning or SFT) is where humans show the model thousands of examples of the format you want it to produce. "Here is a question. Here is a good answer. Be more like this." Fine-tuning uses much less compute than pretraining — you're nudging the existing weights, not building them from nothing — and it can be done in days rather than months. The point of fine-tuning is to teach the model the *form* of being a useful assistant: short responses to short questions, code in code blocks, lists where lists belong, refusals to obviously harmful requests. Fine-tuning is cheap relative to pretraining (single-digit millions, sometimes much less), but it's where most of the model's apparent personality and skill at following instructions comes from.

**RLHF** — reinforcement learning from human feedback — is the third stage and the one that most directly shapes what you experience when you use a frontier model. Humans rank pairs of model outputs ("which of these two responses is better?") and a smaller "reward model" learns to predict the rankings. Then the main model is trained to maximize the reward model's score. RLHF is what makes models stop producing technically-correct but useless answers, what makes them try harder when a question is hard, what makes them refuse certain requests, and what gives Claude its slightly different feel from GPT and Gemini. It is also where most of the *alignment work* happens, in the sense of "making the model behave the way the lab wants." Newer variants — RLAIF (the "AI" versions where another model does the ranking), Constitutional AI, DPO, and others — are refinements on this same step. None of them have replaced the basic loop of train-on-text, then-show-examples, then-rank-and-reinforce.

In 2024, a fourth stage started to matter at the frontier: **post-training reasoning RL**. This is the trick behind reasoning-mode models (OpenAI o1 / o3, Claude 3.7 / 4 with extended thinking, DeepSeek R1, and the rest). The model is trained, via reinforcement learning, to produce long internal chains of thought before answering. This stage can dramatically improve performance on math, code, and logic tasks, sometimes by tens of percentage points on hard benchmarks. It is also expensive in a different way than pretraining: it consumes huge amounts of inference-time compute on hard problems during training, because the model has to actually try things and get scored. The total cost of a frontier reasoning model in 2026 is a stack of all four stages, dominated by pretraining but with the post-training piece growing fast.

Now zoom out. The scaling laws — the empirical observation that loss falls predictably as you increase model size, training data, and compute — are the single most important reason the industry looks the way it does. **They tell you, in advance, that spending 10x more on a training run will produce a measurably better model.** Not might. Will, within a known margin. This is unusual in technology. For most R&D, you spend more money and you don't know what you'll get. For frontier model training, you spend more money and you know roughly what you'll get, and the *roughly* is precise enough that boards of directors approve nine-figure capital expenditures on the strength of curves that have held across four orders of magnitude. That is why the labs raise the capital they raise. That is why a small number of companies can credibly say "if we had $10 billion of compute we would build a model substantially better than today's best." It is not a marketing claim. It is a curve extrapolation.

The two open questions about that curve are exactly the two questions that will determine the next five years. **First: does the curve keep going?** Some recent reports suggest that returns on raw pretraining compute are diminishing — the model improves, but not as much as the curve predicted. If that's real, then the strategy of "spend more, get more" is in trouble, and the labs that bet their roadmaps on it have a problem. **Second: does the new RL-on-reasoning stage open up a fresh curve with its own scaling law?** Early evidence (the gap between non-reasoning and reasoning models on hard benchmarks) suggests yes. If so, the industry has found a second scaling axis to ride and the capex curve continues. If not, the industry is approaching a plateau and the competitive dynamics will reshape entirely, with smaller players catching up because the leaders no longer pull away. Both outcomes are live as of 2026. Anyone who tells you confidently which will win is selling something.

## Concrete example

A useful way to feel the cost curve in your bones: in 2018, the BERT model, which was state-of-the-art for natural language understanding, was trained for under $10,000 in compute on a single beefy machine. By 2020, GPT-3 cost roughly $5 million. By 2023, GPT-4 cost roughly $100 million. By 2026, the rumored figure for the largest frontier runs is north of $1 billion, with several labs publicly claiming they will spend $10 billion on a single training run by 2027. That is six orders of magnitude in eight years, which is a faster cost increase than any other technology I can think of. Notice what this implies for the *number of organizations* that can train a frontier model. In 2018, hundreds of universities could have trained BERT. In 2020, dozens of companies could have trained GPT-3 if they wanted to. In 2026, the number of organizations on earth that can train a model at the absolute frontier is small enough to count on your fingers. Most are in the United States, a few are in China. Universities are no longer in the game at the very top. This consolidation is the most important political fact about AI today, and it is a downstream consequence of one technical fact: the cost curve.

## Uncomfortable question

If frontier model training is converging toward a few-companies, billions-per-run sport, then the overlap between "who can build the best AI" and "who can write nine-figure capex checks" is becoming complete. That is a structural concentration of capability into a tiny set of organizations whose incentives are not democratic and whose decisions affect everyone. The two standard responses are: (a) "this is fine, the labs are run by careful people," and (b) "this is intolerable, AI must be open-sourced and decentralized." Both responses skip the harder question. If the curve continues, and the labs producing the best models are also the only ones who can audit them, what is your actual mechanism for noticing when something has gone wrong? Not your hope. Your mechanism. Most people, when they answer this honestly, find they don't have one.
