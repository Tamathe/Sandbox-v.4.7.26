# Module 3 — Discussion Prompts

Three threaded prompts. Pick at least one to start a thread on, and reply substantively to at least two threads started by others. Quality matters, volume doesn't. See `rubrics/discussion-quality.json` for what "substantive" means here.

---

## Prompt 1 — Was the world computer thesis a useful lie?

Lesson 1 makes the case that Ethereum did not become a world computer in the original sense, but that the original framing recruited the developers and capital that built something different and real. Most foundational technologies are sold on the wrong story before the right story becomes obvious.

**Question for the thread:** Is "useful lie" a defensible description of how technology pitches work, or is it a euphemism for "the founders did not know what they were building"? Pick a side and defend it. If you defend the "useful lie" framing, name another technology where this clearly happened (the internet, smartphones, cloud, AI in 2012). If you reject it, explain what you think the right way to evaluate a 2026 white paper is, given that the 2014 Ethereum pitch was wrong-in-the-specifics and right-in-the-aggregate.

Avoid the temptation to land on "well, both sides have a point" without saying which one matters more.

---

## Prompt 2 — Stablecoins are the use case that won

Lesson 4 argues that stablecoins, not Bitcoin and not ETH, are the largest source of real economic activity in crypto, and that they are technically "fiat in a costume" — mostly tokenized claims on Treasury bills. The Bitcoin maximalists are correct that this is not the revolution they were promised. They are wrong that this means it is unimportant.

**Question for the thread:** If the part of the crypto stack that is doing the most real work is the part that is most embedded in the dollar system, what does that tell you about the original Bitcoin thesis? Three positions are defensible:

(a) It tells us the original thesis was wrong and the world voted with its feet for dollarized programmable money instead of hard digital money.

(b) It tells us nothing about the original thesis, because stablecoins and Bitcoin solve different problems (one is a medium of exchange, the other is a long-term store of value), and both can win.

(c) It tells us the migration to harder money is happening through the gateway of stablecoins — people first learn to hold programmable money in a familiar denomination and then graduate to harder forms of it later.

Pick one. Defend it. Say what evidence over the next five years would make you change your mind.

**Ground rule for this thread:** if you assert that "stablecoins are just fiat" or "Bitcoin is winning anyway," you have to say what *would* count as evidence against your view, in advance, in your post. If you cannot name such evidence, your view is not falsifiable and your post is not contributing.

## Prompt 3 — Where do you draw the decentralization line?

Lesson 6 takes a strong position: a chain is honestly decentralized if and only if it can survive the unilateral hostile action of any single entity, including the entity that originally built it. By that standard, Bitcoin passes, Ethereum mostly passes (with the Lido caveat), Solana probably passes but is unstress-tested, and almost every other production chain in 2026 fails.

**Question for the thread:** Is this the right line, or is it too strict? If you think it is too strict, propose a different line and defend it. If you think it is the right line, name a chain that you currently use or hold that fails this test, and explain why you use or hold it anyway. (Honest answer: most of us have at least one such chain. The point is not to pretend otherwise.)

If you propose a different line, your line has to be falsifiable — you have to be able to say what specific test a chain would fail to be on the wrong side of it. "It feels decentralized" is not a line. "It is run by people I trust" is not a line either, though it is at least an honest non-line.

## How to participate well

- Quote the specific thing you are responding to before responding to it.
- If you are about to type a sentence that contains a slogan ("trustless," "secured by Ethereum," "Web3 is inevitable," "Bitcoin fixes this"), stop and rewrite it without the slogan. If the sentence does not survive the rewrite, the sentence was not load-bearing.
- Naming a specific number (a Nakamoto coefficient, a TVL figure, a transaction-volume figure) is more valuable than naming a vibe. Even an estimate with stated assumptions beats a confident vibe.
- It is fine and good to change your mind in public. The discussion-quality rubric explicitly rewards this.
- It is fine to say "I don't know yet." It is not fine to perform certainty you don't feel.
