# Module 3 — Study Group: Two Chains, Five Honest Dimensions

## Format

Pairs. Each pair will compare two chains they did not write their assignment about, on five honest dimensions, and produce a one-paragraph joint judgment. The point is to use the module's vocabulary on something you have not already written, under time pressure, with a partner who will catch your hand-waving.

## The setup

Before the meeting, each person picks **one chain they want to bring to the table**. It must not be the chain you wrote your assignment about. It can be any chain (other than Bitcoin) that you find interesting, including ones you are skeptical of. You will end up comparing the two chains the pair brings.

## The five dimensions

You will compare your two chains on exactly these five, in this order, with no additions:

1. **Consensus mechanism and what it costs.** PoW, PoS, PoA, something exotic. What is the security model and roughly what is the chain spending per year on security (issuance + fees paid to validators)?
2. **Nakamoto coefficient.** As measured for the most relevant property — usually validators or block producers, sometimes governance. If you cannot get a clean number, say so and give your best estimate with the reasoning.
3. **Censorship surface.** If a US court ordered the chain to freeze a specific address tomorrow, what happens? Who would be on the receiving end of that order? How would the chain respond? "Nothing would happen" is a possible and valid answer for some chains; "the foundation would comply within 24 hours" is a possible and valid answer for others.
4. **Trust assumptions of the largest application running on it.** Pick the biggest app on each chain by TVL or activity. What does that app actually depend on (oracles, upgrade keys, multisigs)? The trust profile of the chain's biggest app is often a more honest description of the chain's real trust model than the chain's own marketing.
5. **The thing the marketing says it is.** Read the chain's home page or documentation landing page. What is the elevator pitch? How does that pitch line up with the four answers above?

## Structure (60 minutes total)

1. **Solo prep (10 min)** — Each person writes down their best one-line answer for each of the five dimensions for the chain they brought. No talking. No phones except to look up the marketing language for dimension 5.
2. **Round 1: Person A presents (10 min)** — Person A walks Person B through their chain on all five dimensions. Person B is required to interrupt at least twice to ask "how do you know that?" or "how would you check?" Performative agreement is failure.
3. **Round 2: Person B presents (10 min)** — Same format, opposite roles. Person A is required to interrupt at least twice.
4. **Joint comparison (15 min)** — Together, fill out a five-row table comparing the two chains side by side on the five dimensions. Where you disagree, write down the disagreement explicitly rather than splitting the difference.
5. **Joint judgment (10 min)** — Together, write a single paragraph (4–6 sentences) answering: *based on the comparison, on which of the five dimensions are these two chains most different, and which chain is doing the more honest job of describing itself?* "Both are equally honest" is not allowed; force a verdict, even a tentative one.
6. **Submit (5 min)** — Post the table and the joint judgment paragraph to the module discussion thread, tagged `#study-group-mod3`.

## Rules

- No phones during the rounds except a single timer.
- You may look up specific numbers during the joint comparison phase, but you cannot use the lookup as a way to avoid disagreeing with your partner. The disagreement is the point.
- When you cannot find a number for the Nakamoto coefficient, write "couldn't find — best estimate X based on Y." That is a perfectly honest answer and is more valuable than a confident wrong number.
- If the two chains turn out to be much more similar than you expected, that is itself an interesting finding. Write it up.
- When writing the joint judgment, you have to *commit*. The forced verdict is the part that makes this exercise teach you something. Splitting the difference is the failure mode.

## Why this format

The temptation in this module, more than any other, is to fence-sit. Every chain has plausible-sounding things to say about itself and an enthusiastic community. The forced five-dimension comparison strips away the room to fence-sit, because the dimensions are concrete and most chains look distinctly worse on at least one of them than they look in the marketing. Doing this with a partner — and with the requirement that the partner interrupts you when you wave your hands — short-circuits the natural drift toward "well, they're both interesting projects." They are not just both interesting projects. They are different systems with different trust models, and the exercise is to see them clearly enough to say which is which.

The other point of pairing is that the chains you and your partner bring will, statistically, not be the chains you would have picked yourselves. You will spend at least 25 minutes seriously engaging with a chain you might not have thought about otherwise. That is the cheapest way to expand your honest map of the space.
