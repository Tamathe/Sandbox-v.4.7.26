# Module 8 — Discussion Prompts

Three threaded prompts. Pick at least one to start a thread on, and reply substantively to at least two threads started by others. Quality matters, volume doesn't. See `rubrics/discussion-quality.json` for what "substantive" means here.

This is the last module's discussion. Most of the people in this thread will have changed in some specific way over the course of the eight modules. The point of these prompts is to surface the change — yours and other people's — and to use the surfacing as the final pass before you write your thesis.

---

## Prompt 1 — The position you have changed on

Lesson 1 of this module argues that frameworks pivot in response to evidence and worldviews flip wholesale. By now you have been through eight modules of pressure on a lot of comfortable answers.

**Question for the thread:** Name the specific position you held at the start of this course that you no longer hold, or hold at a meaningfully different confidence. Be concrete. Not "I'm more open-minded now" — *what* did you change your mind on, *what specifically* moved you, and *what is your new number*?

If your answer is "nothing" — if you walked in with a position and walked out with the same position — sit with that for a moment before you post. It is possible the course had nothing to teach you. It is also possible that you used the eight modules to defend the position you started with. Both are real outcomes; they are very different from each other; and only one of them is the kind of thing you want to be true about yourself.

**Ground rule for this thread:** if you say "I changed my mind," you have to say *what specific thing* you changed your mind about and *what specific thing* moved you. "I changed my mind about Bitcoin" without further detail does not count and other people will (politely) call you on it.

---

## Prompt 2 — The scenario you most under-weight

Lesson 4 sketched three plausible 10-year futures (Hardening, Concentration, Fracturing) with rough probabilities, plus a 5% residual for "weird." The lesson ended with the question: which scenario would you most hate to be right?

**Question for the thread:** Post your own version of the three futures with your own numbers. Then, in two sentences, identify the scenario you most under-weight in your day-to-day behavior — the one you flinch away from — and say what you would do differently if you were taking it as seriously as the numbers suggest you should.

The point of this prompt is not to argue about whose probabilities are correct. They are all wrong in detail; that is fine. The point is to make your model legible and to notice where your *behavior* is inconsistent with your *stated weights*. Almost everyone has this inconsistency. Naming it is half the cure.

**Ground rule for this thread:** the numbers have to sum to no more than 100% (leave residual!) and every named scenario has to have a leading indicator attached. "The Fracturing at 30%" is not enough. "The Fracturing at 30% — leading indicator: more than five distinct stablecoins or CBDCs achieving over $50B in real circulation by 2029" is the form that helps you think clearly.

## Prompt 3 — The thing you will not say in public

Lesson 5 was about the asymmetry between teacher and student and the cost of expressing more confidence than you actually hold. Now the inverse question.

**Question for the thread:** Name one thing you currently believe at high confidence about money, AI, or the macroeconomy that you have decided *not to say in public*, even though you think it is probably true. Not because it is socially awkward — because you know your confidence is higher than the evidence really justifies, and you do not want to spend other people's trust on a number you cannot defend if asked.

This is the hardest of the three prompts and the one that will produce the most revealing posts. The discipline here is unusual: most people, in most discussions, are encouraged to say more of what they believe. This prompt is asking you to publicly identify a belief you are deliberately *under-promoting*, and to explain why. The exercise of writing this down is the cheapest way to find out whether your epistemic discipline survives contact with your own preferences.

**Ground rule for this thread:** no scolding other people for what they post. Some of the things people will name will sound contrarian, uncomfortable, or wrong. The thread only works if people feel safe naming the actual belief they are sitting on. If you disagree, post your own response thread; do not try to litigate other people's confessions in this one.

## How to participate well

- Quote the specific thing you are responding to before responding to it.
- Name your confidence numerically when you make a claim. "I think this is roughly 60%" is more useful than "I believe."
- It is fine — and at this point in the course, expected — to publicly note that you have updated. Updates with reasons attached are the highest-quality contributions in the thread.
- If you are about to type a sentence that contains a slogan, stop and rewrite it without the slogan.
- It is fine to say "I don't know yet." It is not fine to perform certainty you don't feel. By module 8, the people grading the discussion-quality rubric can tell the difference at a glance.
