## Reading

The previous lesson introduced the idea of moving from "I believe X" to "I am 65% on X." This lesson is about why that move is the entire game, where the number 65 actually comes from, and how to stop both ends of the road from collapsing on you — the overconfident end where everything is 95%, and the cowardly end where everything is 50%.

The intellectual scaffolding here is borrowed from Philip Tetlock's *Superforecasting* and the Good Judgment Project. The short version: Tetlock and his collaborators ran a multi-year tournament asking ordinary people to forecast geopolitical and economic events. They scored the forecasts using a metric called the **Brier score**, which rewards being right *and* being calibrated about how right you were. A forecaster who says "70% chance of X" should be right about 70% of the time, across many such forecasts. If they are right 90% of the time, they were systematically underconfident. If they are right 50% of the time, they were systematically overconfident. Both are errors, and both are measurable.

The result that mattered for our purposes: a small group of forecasters — Tetlock called them superforecasters — outperformed not just the average citizen but, in some cases, intelligence analysts with access to classified information. They were not smarter in any IQ sense. What they shared was a *practice*: they updated their probabilities incrementally as new information came in, they were comfortable saying things like "63%" instead of "about two-thirds," they tracked their predictions over time, and they noticed when they were wrong. Calibration, in other words, is trainable. It is closer to a skill than a personality trait.

Now apply that to ideas about money, machines, and markets. Most people in this domain operate at two confidences: 100% and 0%. Bitcoin will replace fiat (100%) or it is a Ponzi (0%). AGI is coming this decade (100%) or it is hype (0%). The Fed has lost control (100%) or it is fine (0%). The actual epistemic state of any honest observer in early 2026 is none of those numbers. It is somewhere in the middle, and the middle is where useful thinking lives.

Here is what **epistemic position sizing** looks like, transferred from portfolio management:

In a portfolio, you do not put 100% of your money into the trade you are most confident in, because you might be wrong, and the cost of being wrong is wiping out. You allocate proportionally to your confidence, with hard limits on how concentrated you let yourself get even on the highest-conviction ideas. The same logic applies to beliefs. *You do not put 100% of your epistemic weight into any one claim about the future*, because you might be wrong, and the cost of being wrong is having your entire model of the world invalidated by a single observation. You hold beliefs in proportion to evidence, with hard limits on how confident you let yourself get even on the highest-conviction claims.

What does this look like in practice? Two examples from inside this course:

- **Claim:** "The dollar will lose more than 30% of its purchasing power over the next ten years." This is a defensible claim. The 2% target compounds to about 22% loss over ten years; recent realized inflation has been higher than target; the political pressure on the Fed is structurally toward leniency. I personally hold this at about 70%. Not 100%, because productivity gains, demographic deflation, and a hawkish Fed reaction are all possible. Not 50%, because the base rates and the political incentives both push the same direction. The number is a *position*, sized to my actual evidence.
- **Claim:** "By 2035, more than half of human knowledge work will be substantially automated by AI agents." This is also a defensible claim, but the uncertainty is much higher. I hold it at maybe 45%. The technology is improving fast, but the integration cost into actual organizations is much higher than the model demos suggest, and "substantially automated" is doing a lot of definitional work. A 45% position is not a flip of a coin; it is "I think this is a real possibility but I would not bet the house on it."

Notice what those two examples have in common. The numbers are specific. They are not "high confidence" or "moderate confidence" — they are 70 and 45. And they are sized to the evidence, not to how I feel that day.

There are two failure modes to watch for, and they are mirror images.

**The overconfidence failure** is the more visible one. It is the person who tweets in absolutes, who treats any uncertainty as weakness, who confuses rhetorical force for epistemic content. This person is usually wrong less often than they should be — the world is in fact patterned, and someone willing to stake out positions will get some of them right. But when they are wrong, they are wrong in big, expensive ways, because they did not size their position to the actual evidence. They had no margin for error. The Bitcoin maximalists who put their entire net worth in at the 2021 top, the AI doomers who quit their jobs in 2023 to prep for extinction, the crypto skeptics who shorted at $20K — all the same failure mode.

**The false-humility failure** is the quieter one. It is the person who hedges everything to 50%, refuses to commit to any number, says "well, who really knows?" about every question, and then mistakes this for epistemic virtue. It is not virtue. It is *cowardice with vocabulary*. If you genuinely believe a claim is 50/50, fine — but you cannot believe *every* claim is 50/50, because the world is not a coin. Real evidence pushes real numbers around. A person whose entire belief system clusters at 50% is not being humble; they are refusing to do the work of looking at evidence, and they are using "I'm just being humble" as cover. The Brier score punishes them just as harshly as the overconfident.

The skill you are trying to build is: **for every interesting claim in this course, have an actual number, and be willing to say what would move it.** You will be wrong about specific numbers. That is fine. You will get better at it the same way you get better at estimating distances by walking around for a few years.

## Concrete example

In late 2024, an analyst I follow — a careful, calibrated person, not a hype merchant — published her probabilities on five specific claims about the next two years. One was: "By end of 2026, at least one G7 central bank will hold Bitcoin as a reserve asset, even in a small experimental quantity." She put this at 25%. Most of her followers either laughed (it's obviously zero) or cheered (it's obviously 100%). Almost none of them had a number of their own to compare. When asked to *bet* against her at her stated odds, the laughers backed off; when asked to bet *with* her at her stated odds, the cheerleaders also backed off. This is the tell. People who are loud at 100% or 0% are usually unwilling to put real stakes against their own implied probability, because they do not actually hold those probabilities — they are performing a tribe membership, and the performance falls apart when you make it expensive.

The exercise of *writing down a number* is the cheapest way to find out whether you actually hold a belief or whether you are just wearing one.

## Uncomfortable question

For each of the three biggest claims you currently believe about Bitcoin, AI, or the macro economy, write down a number between 0 and 100. Then, for each one, write the smallest piece of news that would move the number up by 5 points and the smallest piece of news that would move it down by 5 points. If you cannot do this, you are not holding a position; you are wearing a uniform. If you can, you have done something most people in this domain never do, and the rest of this module will be much easier for you.
