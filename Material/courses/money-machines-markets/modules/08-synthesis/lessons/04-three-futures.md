## Reading

This lesson is the most exposed thing in the course. I am going to sketch three plausible 10-year futures and put rough numbers on them. The numbers will be wrong. That is not a bug. The point of the exercise is not to predict the future; it is to *make my model legible* so that you can disagree with it specifically rather than vaguely. If you walk away from this lesson having silently dismissed my scenarios and not built your own with numbers attached, you have missed the point. The next module assignment will force you to do exactly that.

A note on the format. I am writing three scenarios, not five or ten, because three is the minimum number that forces you to commit to a view (two collapses to a binary; four lets you hedge into mush) and the maximum number a normal brain can hold simultaneously. The probabilities I assign sum to 95%, with a deliberate **5% residual for "weird"** — black swans, things none of the three scenarios saw coming. Always leave residual probability. A scenario set that sums to exactly 100% is hiding overconfidence in its own completeness.

### Scenario A — The Hardening (≈25%)

In this future, the long arc of the 2020s monetary expansion produces enough visible damage — sovereign debt strain, repeated inflationary episodes, declining trust in central bank communication — that hard-money assets accumulate a serious monetary premium. Bitcoin reaches the point where at least one G7 central bank holds it as a reserve asset (even symbolically), several mid-sized states adopt it as legal tender alongside their local currency, and stablecoin float exceeds physical dollar circulation globally. Gold also rallies. AI capability continues to improve, but a combination of regulation, energy constraints, and a real plateau in returns to scale slows the most disruptive scenarios. The economy is recognizably a continuation of the present, but the *plumbing* of money has been rewired.

**Leading indicator:** the percentage of cross-border B2B settlements that route through stablecoin rails. If that number passes 10% of global cross-border flow before 2030, the Hardening is underway. If it stays below 3%, this scenario is dying.

**What it requires being right about:** the durability of Bitcoin's network, the political cost of further fiat debasement, the assumption that sovereigns adopt rather than ban.

### Scenario B — The Concentration (≈45%)

In this future, the dominant story of the next decade is not money — it is *intelligence*. AI capability continues to scale, and the economic returns to having access to the frontier compound until three or four firms (the obvious names plus one or two surprises) are the de facto operating system of knowledge work. Most of the value generated by AI accrues to the platform layer, not to the application layer or the open-source commons. Crypto persists but becomes a niche store-of-value play and a settlement layer for AI-to-AI commerce, rather than the mainstream monetary alternative its proponents hoped for. Fiat persists, central banks retain credibility (with some bumps), and the most consequential political fights of the late 2020s and early 2030s are about *AI labor displacement and rent extraction by platforms*, not about monetary policy. Bitcoin is a successful asset class but not a successful currency. AI is the protagonist of the decade.

**Leading indicator:** the gross margins and pricing power of the top three frontier model labs. If they expand and entrench, the Concentration is happening. If new entrants compress them or open-source catches up, the scenario weakens.

**What it requires being right about:** that scaling laws keep producing usable capability, that regulation does not break the leading labs, that the integration cost of AI into knowledge work falls fast enough to actually displace rather than augment.

I weight this the highest because it is, broadly, an extrapolation of the trends most clearly visible in early 2026. Extrapolation is the laziest way to forecast. It is also the way to bet if you have no better model, because most decades are continuations of the trends visible at their start. The Concentration scenario is the modal future, which is exactly why I trust it the least in any specific detail.

### Scenario C — The Fracturing (≈25%)

In this future, there is no single dominant story. Geopolitics, capital controls, energy constraints, and competing technological sovereignties produce a *multipolar* monetary and computational landscape. The dollar persists as the largest reserve currency but its share continues to decline; the renminbi, the euro, gold-backed BRICS instruments, Bitcoin, and several major stablecoins all coexist as partial reserves and partial settlement layers depending on jurisdiction. AI capability is real but distributed unevenly: the US frontier is strongest, China's is close behind, the EU regulates aggressively, the global south gets capable but cheaper open-weight models. There is no clear winner of either the money fight or the intelligence fight, and most of the political energy of the decade goes into *managing the friction between systems that do not interoperate well*. The world feels less coherent, not more. Some people do extremely well in this environment by being early to specific niches; most people feel a vague sense that the rules are being rewritten and they cannot tell which rules.

**Leading indicator:** the number of distinct stablecoins or CBDCs that achieve more than $50B in real circulation. One or two means the Concentration is winning. Five or more means the Fracturing is winning.

**What it requires being right about:** that the political will to maintain a unipolar financial system keeps eroding, that no single AI lab achieves decisive technical lead, that the world keeps splintering rather than converging.

### Residual — Weird (≈5%)

This is the bucket for everything none of the three scenarios above contemplate. Some examples, none of which I'm endorsing, just to make the bucket concrete: a major central bank quietly defaults on its sovereign and the entire reserve system reorganizes inside 18 months; a frontier AI model genuinely automates a critical industry inside three years and the political reaction is more violent than anyone expected; a Bitcoin protocol-level failure (technical or social) that I am not currently modeling; an unrelated crisis (war, climate shock, pandemic) that reshuffles the deck so completely that none of these scenarios survive contact with it. The reason to keep the residual nonzero is that *every decade in living memory has had at least one event that was in nobody's scenario set at the beginning of it*. Pretending you can enumerate all the possibilities is a much bigger error than admitting you cannot.

### What to do with this

Three things, in order of importance.

**Build your own version.** Mine are not yours. Sit with each one for ten minutes, write your own probabilities, write your own leading indicators. The numbers will look different. That is exactly what should happen. The point of this exercise is to make your model legible *to you*, so you can update it deliberately rather than drift between vibes.

**Make decisions consistent with your weighted average.** If your numbers are roughly Hardening 30, Concentration 40, Fracturing 25, Weird 5, then your behavior should reflect a *blend* of those scenarios, not the one you find most ideologically attractive. People who put all their behavioral weight into the scenario they want to be true are the people who get hurt the worst when it isn't.

**Update slowly and visibly.** Keep your numbers in a file somewhere. Look at them every six months. Notice when they have moved and ask why. If they have moved a lot and you cannot point to specific evidence that justifies the move, you have been narrative-captured by something and the previous lesson on information diet is the place to start fixing it.

## Concrete example

In late 2019, a small group of analysts I read had something close to a "concentration" scenario for AI on the table — they believed that compute scaling would be the dominant story of the decade. Their probability on it was something like 35%. They were embarrassed by it at the time, because most people in their professional circle thought the scaling-pilled view was naive. By 2024, they had updated to something like 60%, but they had not updated to 90%, even though the public conversation had. Their reason: the *political and integration friction* still hadn't been tested at the scale that would matter, and a 60% bet on the scenario being right is meaningfully different from a 90% bet in terms of how much of your life you reorganize around it. Watching them update incrementally, in public, with reasons attached, was the best example I've ever seen of scenario thinking working as designed. Most of the people who were right about the *direction* were wrong about the *size*, and the size is what determined whether they made or lost money on the conviction.

## Uncomfortable question

Of the three scenarios above, which one would you most hate to be right? Not which one you think is least likely — which one would, if it came true, force you to admit you were wrong about something you have publicly committed to. That scenario is the one you are most likely to under-weight without noticing. Your job, for the rest of this module, is to *over-correct* for that bias by deliberately spending extra time on its leading indicators. The goal is not to hope it doesn't happen. The goal is to be ready to notice it if it does.
