## Reading

If you have made it through the first seven modules of this course, you already have one problem this lesson will not solve: there is too much being written about Bitcoin, blockchains, AI, and the macro economy for any human to keep up with, and most of it is bad. The lesson is about how to deal with that fact without either drowning in it or shutting it out.

Start with the wrong frame, so we can throw it away: "What should I read?" That is the wrong question. The right question is: **what stack of sources, in what proportion, with what cadence, will leave me better calibrated in six months than I am today?** That is an information *diet*, not a reading list. A reading list is a pile. A diet is a structure.

Three principles, then a stack.

**First: rank sources by track record, not by how they make you feel.** This is the single hardest discipline in the entire lesson, and it is the one most people cannot stomach. The sources that make you feel smart, validated, and at home in the world are *almost always* worse signals than the sources that irritate you. This is not because irritation is a sign of truth — it isn't, plenty of irritating people are wrong — but because *feeling validated* is a sign that the source is selecting for what you already believe, and selection bias is the silent killer of calibration. You are looking for sources whose predictions you can score, not sources whose vibes match yours.

How do you rank a source by track record? You pick five things they said clearly two years ago, and you check what happened. Most popular commentators, when you do this exercise, fall apart immediately. They did not say anything clearly. They said vague directional things that could be interpreted either way. The good sources are the ones who said specific things, dated them, and either turned out to be right or *publicly noted when they were wrong*. The "noted when they were wrong" is half of the signal. A source with a 60% hit rate that publicly tracks itself is more useful than a source with a 70% hit rate that quietly memory-holes its misses.

**Second: prefer primary sources over commentary, and prefer commentary that links to primary sources.** A Fed statement is a primary source. A Bitcoin Core release note is a primary source. An OpenAI system card is a primary source. The same information filtered through three layers of pundits is mostly noise, with a thin layer of analysis on top that you could have done yourself. Yes, the primary sources are dry. Yes, they take longer. The dryness is the price of avoiding being told what to think. Read fewer things, more carefully, closer to the source. The 80/20 here is brutal: 80% of the analytical commentary you could be reading is not worth your time, and you will not lose anything by ignoring it.

**Third: maintain a deliberate mute list, and treat it as seriously as your read list.** What you do not read shapes you almost as much as what you do. The mute list is not a list of "people I disagree with." (You should read people you disagree with — see below.) The mute list is a list of *sources whose update behavior makes you worse calibrated*. These are usually the high-volume, high-confidence, low-track-record voices that move your emotional state without giving you any new information. Anyone whose every piece of content makes you feel either euphoric or panicked is on the wrong side of this line. Mute them. Not because they are bad people. Because you cannot afford the noise.

Now the stack. This is roughly what I run, for context. Your version will look different and that is fine.

- **Three to five primary sources**, read on their own publication cadence. For monetary policy: the FOMC statements and minutes. For Bitcoin: the BIP repository and one well-curated newsletter that summarizes it. For AI: the major lab system cards and the arXiv categories you actually understand. For macro: the BIS quarterly review. None of these are exciting. All of them are *upstream* of the things you read in commentary.
- **Two or three analytical sources** with a track record you can score. People who put dated, specific predictions on the record and update them publicly. If you cannot find their old predictions, that is a signal.
- **One or two contrarian sources** whose conclusions you think are wrong but whose reasoning you cannot easily dismiss. These are the most valuable items in the stack. They are the people who, if they turn out to be right, will cost you the most. Read them slowly. Steel-man them as you go.
- **A mute list** of high-volume voices whose content has not made you better calibrated in the last six months. Reassess the mute list quarterly. Some people earn their way back on; some people on the read list earn their way off.
- **A "once a month" cadence** for genuinely long-form analysis (a book chapter, a long essay), separate from the daily flow. Most people fail at this not because they cannot find long-form material but because they let the daily flow eat the time.

Two things this stack does not do, and that is deliberate.

It does not include real-time price feeds, alerts, or anything that will buzz at you. Real-time information is almost always a worse input to your beliefs than the same information processed an hour later, because the noise-to-signal ratio in real-time data on these topics is brutal and your nervous system is not built to handle it. If you genuinely need to react in real time, you are a trader, and this is not a trading course; if you are not a trader, real-time data costs you sleep without buying you anything.

It does not include "everything important," because the goal is *not* to be aware of everything. The goal is to be reliably not-wrong about the things that matter most for your view of the world, and to know what you do not know. A person who reads ten things deeply and acknowledges their blind spots is a better thinker than a person who consumes a hundred things shallowly and pretends to a coverage they do not have.

A note on **update whiplash**. This is the failure mode where you read something convincing on Monday, swing your view 30 points, read the rebuttal on Wednesday, swing it back 35 points, and end up exhausted, more cynical, and convinced that nothing can be known. Whiplash is almost always a sign that you are reading sources at the wrong cadence — too frequent, too emotionally charged, and too thin on actual evidence. A well-designed information diet produces small, slow updates, and any week in which you swing more than 10 points on a major view should be a signal to slow down and ask whether the new evidence really justified the move or whether the new piece was simply better written than the old one.

## Concrete example

A friend who runs money for a living has a rule I have stolen and never regretted: he does not read any market commentary written less than 24 hours after the event it describes. Not because the commentary is wrong — sometimes it is fine — but because the *act* of reading hot takes shapes his nervous system in ways that bleed into his decisions for the rest of the week. He reads the next morning's coverage instead of the live blog. He reads the weekend long-form instead of the Wednesday hot take. He has, as far as I can tell, never missed an important development by doing this, and his calibration on the things he does read is much better than mine.

The discipline is not "read less news." It is "read news after the noise has settled out of it." That is a different and more useful rule, and it generalizes from markets to almost everything in this course.

## Uncomfortable question

Open the last 20 pieces of content you actively consumed about money, machines, or markets. For each one, ask: did this make me better calibrated about anything specific, or did it just make me feel something? Be honest. Most people, the first time they do this exercise, find that 16 of the 20 fall in the "made me feel something" bucket. If your number is similar, your information diet is not feeding you; it is grooming you. The next move is yours.
