## Reading

Seven modules in, you have most of the vocabulary you need to think clearly about Bitcoin, blockchains, AI, and the macroeconomic terrain they are crashing into. What you do not yet have — and what this module exists to give you — is a *framework* for using that vocabulary without quietly joining a tribe.

Let me define the distinction, because it is the most important thing I will say in this module.

**A worldview is a set of answers. A framework is a set of questions.** A worldview tells you what is true. A framework tells you how to decide what is true when a new claim shows up. Tribes hand out worldviews for free, pre-packaged, with the social validation included. Frameworks you have to build yourself, and they are lonelier, because they will not let you nod along with the people in your timeline.

Why are tribes so seductive in this space specifically? Because every domain we have studied — sound money, blockchain, AI, central banking — is *high-stakes, high-uncertainty, and high-status-volatile*. In domains like that, the cost of "I don't know" is not zero. It is reputational. People look at you funny. You are not a player. You are not "in the trade." The fastest way to make that discomfort go away is to pick a side, learn its talking points, and outsource your thinking to whoever runs the side. Bitcoin maximalists, e/acc accelerationists, AI doomers, regulated-finance institutionalists, the gold bugs, the Modern Monetary Theory people — every one of these is offering you the same product: certainty in exchange for loyalty. It is a fair trade if you want certainty more than you want to be right. If you want to be *right*, it is the worst trade you can make in this field.

The framework I am going to suggest is built around four questions that you ask any new claim before you let it into your model of the world. They are deliberately boring. The boringness is the point.

1. **What is the claim, exactly?** Restate it in your own words, without slogans. If the claim survives only when wrapped in a slogan ("inflation is theft," "AGI is inevitable," "Bitcoin fixes this"), you have not found the claim yet. Keep going.
2. **Who would have to be wrong for this claim to be true, and are they actually wrong?** Every interesting claim implies that some group of people who have thought about it carefully are mistaken. Identify them by name if you can. Read what they actually say, not what their critics say they say. If the answer is "no smart person has ever held the opposite view," you are probably looking at a strawman version of the question.
3. **What would have to be observable in the world for this claim to be wrong?** A claim that cannot be falsified by any future observation is not a claim about the world; it is a claim about your tribe. This question is the most painful one. People resist it because it forces them to commit to giving up something. That is exactly why it is the most useful.
4. **How confident am I, on a 0–100 scale, and why that number and not five points higher or lower?** We will spend the entire next lesson on this question. For now, just notice: the move from "I believe X" to "I am 65% on X" is the move from a worldview to a framework. It is the price of admission to thinking carefully.

A framework built out of those four questions does something specific that a tribal worldview does not: it produces *gradients*, not flips. You will end up holding most claims at some intermediate confidence, and you will update them by small amounts as new information arrives. You will not have the experience, common in tribes, of being suddenly "converted" or suddenly "redpilled" or suddenly "blackpilled." Conversions are a sign that someone was running a worldview, not a framework. A framework does not flip. It pivots, slowly, in response to evidence.

The cost of this is real. You will have less to say at parties. You will lose arguments to people who are more certain than you, because confidence is rhetorically powerful even when it is empirically empty. You will be less fun on Twitter. The compensating benefit is that you will be wrong less often in the ways that actually cost you money, time, or relationships. That trade has, in my experience, been overwhelmingly worth it. You may decide otherwise. It is a real choice.

One final thing. Steel-manning — the practice of restating your opponent's position in the strongest possible form before attacking it — is not a courtesy. It is a debugging tool. The strongest form of the opposing view is the version that, if it turns out to be right, would cost you the most. That is exactly the version you need to know in order to position yourself properly. Treating steel-manning as politeness misses the point. It is selfish, in the best sense: you are doing it because *you* need the information that only the strongest version of the other side contains.

## Concrete example

A friend of mine — sharp, well-read, decent investor — spent most of 2021 telling me Bitcoin was a Ponzi scheme. He spent most of 2024 telling me Bitcoin was the future of money. Same person. Same brain. Different tribe. When I asked him what evidence had moved him, he could not name anything specific: not a paper, not a chart, not an experience. He had simply moved to a new social environment in which the people he respected held the new view, and his views had migrated to match. He was running a worldview, and the worldview had been replaced wholesale. Nothing in his framework had been updated, because there was no framework to update.

I do not say this to mock him. Most people do exactly this and never notice. The reason this lesson is the first of the synthesis module is that the rest of the module — calibration, information diet, three futures, ethics of evangelism — is downstream of this one move: *stop running a worldview, start running a framework.* If you do not make that move, none of the rest of the module will help you, because you will simply use the new techniques to defend the latest tribe you joined.

## Uncomfortable question

Pick the position in this course that you currently find most uncomfortable. Not the one that is most obviously wrong to you — the one that, if it turned out to be right, would force the biggest change in how you live or invest. Now, in three sentences, write the strongest possible version of that position, as if you were trying to convince a smart skeptic. If you cannot write those three sentences without sneering, you have not steel-manned it yet, and you are running a worldview where you think you are running a framework. Try again until you can write the three sentences clean.
