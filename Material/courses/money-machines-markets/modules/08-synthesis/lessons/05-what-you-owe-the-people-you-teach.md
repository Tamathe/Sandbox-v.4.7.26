## Reading

Almost everyone who finishes this course is going to end up explaining some piece of it to someone else. Not because the course expects you to — it doesn't — but because that is what humans do with hard-won mental models. You will be at a dinner, or in a Slack, or on a call with your parents, and the conversation will arrive at *what is going on with Bitcoin* or *should I be worried about AI* or *why has my rent gone up faster than my paycheck*, and you will know more than the people in the room. At that moment, you will be a teacher, whether you intended to be or not.

This last lesson is about what you owe the people you teach. It is the most important lesson in the module and the one with the least technical content, which is exactly why I have put it last. Everything in the previous four lessons — frameworks, calibration, information diet, scenario planning — was about making *you* a clearer thinker. This lesson is about the responsibility that comes with being clearer than the people around you, and the specific ways that responsibility is most often betrayed.

Start with the asymmetry. **When you teach someone, you are not a peer.** Even if you and the listener are professionally equal, even if they are smarter than you in ten other domains, in this specific moment they are deferring to your model. They will weight your statements more heavily than a neutral source would. They will remember your tone more than your hedges. They will leave the conversation having absorbed not just the *content* of what you said but also the *confidence* with which you said it. If you said it at 95% confidence and the truth is 60%, they will walk away holding a 95% belief and you will have made the world less calibrated by a small amount. Multiply that across many conversations, and you can do real damage without ever saying anything that was technically wrong.

This is what I mean by **epistemic trust debt**. Every time you express more certainty than you actually hold, you are spending the listener's trust on something you did not earn. The debt is invisible to both of you in the moment. It comes due when the future does not match the prediction, and the listener is left holding a confident belief that has been falsified, with no framework for updating it because they never had a framework — they only had your confidence. Some of them will react by quietly losing faith in you and never telling you. Some will react by digging in harder, because admitting they were wrong would mean admitting they outsourced their thinking. Either way, the damage is done.

Now the tempting move, which I want you to refuse: the answer to this is *not* to retreat into "everything is uncertain, who can say." That move is also a betrayal, and it is the one that intellectually-cautious people make instead of the overconfidence trap. **False humility is a way of refusing responsibility for what you actually believe.** If someone you care about is about to make a big decision — buying a house, taking a job, putting their savings somewhere — and you mumble "well, who really knows" because you do not want to be on the hook, you have not protected them. You have abandoned them. They were going to make the decision anyway. They came to you because they wanted *your actual best estimate*, calibrated and honest, not a dodge.

The honest position is in between, and it is harder than either of the easy moves. It is something like this: *Here is what I think, here is how confident I am, here is what would change my mind, and here is what I would do if I were in your shoes — but the choice is yours and the responsibility for the outcome is yours.* That is the calibrated version of teaching. It is harder to deliver than either confident evangelism or hedged mumbling, because it requires you to have actually done the work of holding a calibrated view. You cannot fake it. The people you talk to can tell.

Three concrete habits, in descending order of importance.

**The first habit: never be more confident in conversation than you are in your private notes.** If you have a number on a claim in your own head — say, 65% — you are not allowed to round it up to 90% in the telling because the audience finds 65% unsatisfying. This is the most common failure mode, and it is the one that does the most damage. The fix is to *practice* saying intermediate confidences out loud until they feel natural. "I think this is more likely than not, maybe 60-something percent, but I want to flag that I am not very confident." Most of your listeners have never heard a number like that in conversation about politics or markets. Some of them will laugh at you. Some of them will, for the first time, realize that thinking carefully about the world feels different from picking a team. You are doing both groups a service.

**The second habit: refuse the sale.** When you find yourself getting good at explaining a position, and the person across from you starts to look convinced, *the temptation to push them over the line is real and you must resist it*. The line between explaining a thing well and recruiting someone to a tribe is thinner than it feels in the moment. The test is simple: ask yourself whether you would be equally happy if the person *understood* your view but did not adopt it. If the answer is no — if you find yourself wanting them to come over to your side — you are no longer teaching, you are evangelizing, and the other lesson in this module ("don't join a tribe") applies in reverse. Don't *recruit* to one either. The goal is more clear thinkers in the world. It is not more members of your team.

**The third habit: apply the recommend test before making any specific recommendation about money, jobs, or technology.** The recommend test is: *if this person follows my advice, and the worst plausible scenario plays out, can I look them in the eye in five years and explain why I gave the advice?* Not "was I unlucky," not "the data looked good at the time" — can I genuinely defend the advice as reasonable given what I knew? If the answer is no, do not give the recommendation. Give them the framework instead, and let them make the call. This is not cowardice. It is the recognition that *advice has an expected value to the giver and a variance to the receiver*, and the variance is not yours to spend.

Now the part that I expect to be most controversial. There is a version of "teaching" in this domain that is really just a pyramid of overconfidence: the influencer who is 95% certain because the previous influencer they learned from was 95% certain, who was 95% certain because the original source was 95% certain, who was usually selling something. Each layer of the pyramid loses information about the original uncertainty and adds a layer of social conviction. The result is that by the time the message reaches a normal person at a dinner party, it has been laundered into something like a fact, and the chain of reasoning that produced it has been lost three layers up. **You do not want to be a layer in that pyramid.** The way to not be a layer in it is to *cite your uncertainty as loudly as you cite your conclusions*, every time, to everyone, even when it is socially expensive. Especially when it is socially expensive.

I want to end with the thing I most believe and have most often failed at. The reason to be careful with the people you teach is not abstract. It is this: somebody you care about is going to make a real decision based on something you said in a conversation you have already forgotten. You will not be there when they make it. The version of you that exists in their head — the one they trust, the one they remember saying confident things — is going to be the one in the room at the moment of decision. **You owe that version of you to be honest, calibrated, and humble enough to be wrong out loud.** Anything less, and you have used their trust against them.

## Concrete example

A friend who teaches economics to undergraduates told me once that the moment he changed his teaching style was when a former student emailed him, five years after graduation, to thank him for "convincing me that the gold standard would have prevented 2008." He had said no such thing. He had spent one lecture *steel-manning* the gold standard position before tearing it down in the next lecture, and the student had only attended the first one. The student had walked out into the world, into a real life, with a confident belief that my friend had never actually held — and the student trusted the belief because *it had come from his professor*. The professor had been entirely careful in his own head. He had not been careful enough about how the careful version *transmitted*. The lesson he took, and that I have stolen, is that the audience hears the *loudest* line in your presentation, not the *truest* one. The asymmetry of memory is doing a lot of work, and the only defense is to make the truest line also the loudest one.

## Uncomfortable question

Think of the most recent time you confidently told someone something about money, AI, or the macroeconomy. Reconstruct the conversation as honestly as you can. Were you, in your private head, holding the claim at the same confidence you projected in the room? If not — if you rounded 65 up to 90 because the room wanted an answer — what specifically caused you to round up? Social discomfort? The desire to seem smart? An audience that didn't want hedges? Whatever the answer is, that is the failure mode you are going to have to manage *every time you teach this material* for the rest of your life. Knowing the name of your particular failure mode is more than half the cure.
