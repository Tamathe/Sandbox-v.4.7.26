## Reading

For 200 years the story of automation has been a story about *physical* work. Looms replaced weavers. Tractors replaced field hands. Robotic welders replaced auto workers. The pattern was consistent enough that economists built a comfortable framework around it: machines take over the physical and routine tasks, humans move up the value chain into cognitive and creative work, total employment grows, wages rise on the cognitive side, and society on net becomes richer. The losers are real and they hurt, but the long arc bends toward more jobs, not fewer. This story is the labor-economics consensus from roughly 1830 to roughly 2022.

Then large language models got good. And the part of the story that said "humans move up into cognitive work" started looking very different, because the new machines move up with them.

I want to be careful here. I am not going to tell you that "AI will replace all knowledge work by 2030," because that claim is unfalsifiable in the strong form and almost certainly wrong in the weak form. I am also not going to tell you "AI is just a productivity tool that will create more jobs than it destroys," because that claim is the lazy default of people who have not actually used the current models. The honest answer is somewhere in between, and the shape of the in-between matters more than which slogan you adopt.

Here is the framework. Every job is a bundle of tasks. Some of those tasks are *substitutable* by AI — the AI does them at lower marginal cost than the human and the human's role on those tasks shrinks or vanishes. Other tasks are *complementary* to AI — the AI makes the human dramatically more effective at them, and the human becomes more valuable, not less. The interesting question for any particular job is the *ratio* of substitutable to complementary tasks, and what the human is left holding when the substitutable ones get peeled off.

Take a junior associate at a law firm. Around 2022, that role consisted heavily of document review, contract markup, legal research, summarizing case law, and drafting first-pass memos. By 2026, all of those tasks can be done by an AI tool with senior-attorney supervision in a fraction of the time, at a fraction of the cost. The law firm still needs senior attorneys to make judgment calls, manage client relationships, argue in court, and bear professional responsibility. It needs *fewer* junior attorneys, because the work that used to train juniors by repetition is now done by software. The junior career path narrows. The senior path remains. The path between them gets harder to walk because the rungs of the ladder are missing.

Take a graphic designer producing logos for small businesses. Around 2022, that role consisted of about ten hours of work per logo at $500-1500 per logo. By 2026, the small business can ask a tool for ten options in two minutes and pick one for $20. The graphic designer still has clients who want bespoke, high-touch, brand-led work — but the floor of the market has fallen out. There is now no entry-level price point for new designers to build a portfolio.

Take a senior software engineer. Around 2022, that role consisted of about 60% writing code, 30% reviewing code and architecture, and 10% communicating with humans. By 2026, the writing-code share has dropped from 60% to maybe 20%, because the AI writes the first pass and the human edits. The reviewing and human-communication shares have grown proportionally. The total amount of software produced per engineer is up sharply. Whether the *number of engineers employed* is up or down depends on whether software demand grows faster than per-engineer productivity, which it has done for 70 years and may or may not continue to do.

Three patterns in those examples worth noticing. First, the impact is not "destroy the job." It is *reshape what's left of the job and remove the bottom rung*. Second, the most disrupted workers are not the lowest-skilled workers (the cliché about robots taking factory jobs) but the *middle* — the educated, credentialed, white-collar entry level. This is a reversal of every previous wave of automation. It will play out very differently politically, because the people losing ground are the people who have always assumed they were on the right side of the technology curve. Third, the *senior* version of each role often becomes more valuable, not less. The ladder is getting steeper at the top and shorter at the bottom. The "missing middle" is the thing to watch.

Two important caveats from the AI side. **Polanyi's paradox**: there are things humans know how to do but cannot articulate well enough to teach to a machine. ("I can recognize my mother's face but cannot explain how.") Tacit knowledge is sticky; many real jobs are mostly tacit knowledge dressed up in a few articulated tasks. **Moravec's paradox**: the things that are hard for humans (high-end abstract reasoning, chess, formal proofs) have turned out to be relatively easy for machines, and the things that are easy for humans (pouring coffee, walking on uneven ground, reading a room) have turned out to be very hard for machines. The implication is unsettling for the standard "move up the value chain" advice: the economically *least* threatened jobs over the next decade may be plumbers and electricians and elder-care workers, and the *most* threatened may be the upper-middle-class cognitive jobs we have spent two generations telling kids to aspire to.

What does this do to wages? In the short run, cognitive-task wages compress. The senior end of cognitive work probably *rises* (because the few people who can manage AI-augmented teams of one become much more productive). The junior end falls or disappears. The variance widens. The premium that used to exist for "I have a college degree and can write a competent memo" shrinks to nothing because writing a competent memo is now free. The new premium attaches to *taste* (knowing which output is good), *judgment* (knowing which problem to solve), and *trust* (the fiduciary relationships that you cannot offload to a model because legal liability is human-shaped). These are real, valuable, hard-to-fake skills. They are also not skills you can train someone in by giving them a credential. They develop through apprenticeship and reps, and the apprenticeship pipeline is exactly what the AI tools just disrupted.

Tie this to Module 1. Money is, among other things, a measure of relative scarcity. When cognition becomes a cheap input, anything priced primarily on cognitive labor becomes cheap, and anything priced on human-only inputs (taste, trust, attention, physical presence) gets relatively *more* expensive. Watch the price ratios. The price of writing one more email is collapsing toward zero. The price of getting one hour of a credible expert's actual attention is rising. That ratio is the labor market repricing in real time, and most public discussion of "AI and jobs" is a generation behind the curve because it is still arguing about whether the robots will come for us, instead of looking at which tasks they have already taken.

## Concrete example

A US-based mid-sized accounting firm I'll call Firm X had about 240 employees in 2022. In 2024 they deployed an AI tool that automated about 70% of their tax-return preparation work. By the end of 2025, they had 195 employees — and revenue was up about 30% over 2022. They didn't fire anyone; they simply stopped hiring at the bottom and let attrition do the work. The remaining 195 employees were on average more senior, more expensive per head, and more productive per head. The total wage bill was nearly flat. The owners' margins were sharply higher. The local business school's graduating accounting majors had a much harder time finding entry-level positions. None of this shows up cleanly in the unemployment rate, because *there were no layoffs*. It showed up in something subtler: the absence of a starting line for the next generation. This is what AI's labor impact actually looks like. Not robots marching workers out the door. Job listings that quietly stop getting posted.

## Uncomfortable question

You almost certainly have at least one task in your job that an AI can already do better than you, faster than you, at a fraction of the cost. Pick one, honestly. (Not the easy ones — pick something you actually take pride in.) Now imagine that in three years your employer figures this out. What would you do differently *this quarter* to make sure you are not the person the AI replaces? "Learn more AI" is not an answer; everyone is already saying it. The serious version of the question is: which of the three things — taste, judgment, trust — are you actually building in your current role, and what evidence would you point to that you are building it? If the honest answer is "I'm not building any of those, I'm just executing tasks faster than before," then you have just located the thing you should be most worried about, and the most actionable lever you have.
