## Reading

Module 1 ended with a question: what does it mean for money to be "soft," and why does anyone care? This module starts where that question leads in the real world. Across the developed economies, the soft-money system built after 1971 has been doing a specific thing for the last 50 years, and that thing is now hitting limits that almost everyone in policy can see and almost nobody in policy will name out loud.

The thing it has been doing is borrowing.

Here is the long arc, compressed. After WWII, the United States and most of its allies had very high public debt — the US peaked around 119% of GDP in 1946 — incurred to fight the war. That debt was then ground down over the next 30 years through a combination of economic growth, modest inflation, and **financial repression** (interest rates held below the inflation rate, capital controls limiting where savers could go). By the early 1970s, US debt-to-GDP had fallen to around 35%. This is the great post-war success story you have heard about. Note what made it possible: real growth of 3-4% per year, a young and growing population, and a Bretton Woods monetary order that pegged dollars to gold and other currencies to dollars. The debt got smaller because the denominator got bigger and the numerator was held still while inflation quietly ate it.

Then 1971 happened. Nixon closed the gold window. The Bretton Woods system ended. Currencies began to float. The constraint that had quietly disciplined fiscal policy — *if you print too much, gold flows out* — was gone. From 1971 onward, the only constraints on sovereign borrowing were political and economic, not mechanical.

What happened next? In the United States, debt-to-GDP bottomed in 1974 at about 31% and has gone almost monotonically up since. It crossed 50% in the early 1990s, 70% during the 2008 crisis, 100% during the 2020 pandemic, and was sitting around 122-125% by the start of 2026. France, Italy, Japan, and the UK look similar or worse. Japan is the extreme case at over 250%. The pattern is universal across developed democracies: every crisis ratchets the number up, and during the calm years between crises the number does not come back down. The ratchet only goes one way.

Now, you have probably heard people say "debt-to-GDP doesn't matter for a country that issues its own currency, because it can always print to pay." That statement is half right and very dangerous. It is true that a sovereign in its own currency cannot be *forced* into nominal default the way a household or a corporation can. It is not true that this means there are no consequences. The consequences just show up in a different place: in the *currency*, not in the *debt*. This is what economists mean by **real default**. You don't tell the bondholders you can't pay. You pay them in full, in a currency that has lost half its purchasing power. The bondholders get the number of dollars they were promised. They do not get the value those dollars represented when they made the loan. This is default by inflation, and historically it is by far the most common way large sovereigns deal with unsustainable debt loads. It is politically much cheaper than the alternatives. Nobody has to vote for it. The technical word for it is "inflating away" the debt; the honest word for it is real default in slow motion.

This brings us to **fiscal dominance**, the concept that does the most work in this module. Normally, monetary policy is set by the central bank to manage inflation, and fiscal policy is set by the legislature to manage spending. They are nominally independent. Fiscal dominance is the regime in which fiscal needs *override* monetary independence — the central bank can no longer raise rates to fight inflation, because doing so would make the government's interest payments unsustainable. At that point, the central bank stops being a free agent. It becomes, in effect, a debt-management arm of the Treasury. Inflation gets tolerated because the alternative — a debt crisis — is worse for the people in the room. Critically, the central bank does not announce that it has entered fiscal dominance. The shift happens by drift, not by decree. You only know in retrospect.

Are we there yet? Reasonable economists disagree. The case that we are: US interest payments on the federal debt crossed $1 trillion per year in 2024 and are now the second-largest line item in the federal budget after Social Security, larger than defense; every 1% rise in average interest rates adds roughly $300 billion per year to that bill within a decade; the Fed has demonstrably loosened policy in 2024-25 with inflation still above target. The case that we are not: the Treasury market still functions, foreign demand for dollars is still healthy, the dollar's reserve currency status gives the US fiscal capacity that no other country has, and the Fed has held the line on more than one occasion when many predicted it would fold. Both of these stories are coherent. The honest answer is: closer than we were a decade ago, not yet undeniable, and the trajectory matters more than the level.

Why does any of this matter for a course about Bitcoin and AI? Because almost every other thread in this module — energy costs, capital concentration, the CBDC vs stablecoin fight, the demographic squeeze on entitlements — runs *into* the sovereign debt question. The fiscal arithmetic conditions everything else. A government that has to roll $9 trillion of short-term debt every year cannot tolerate a sustained recession that crashes tax receipts, cannot tolerate a sustained spike in interest rates, and cannot tolerate a financial crisis that requires a bailout it can't fund. Its options narrow. Its tolerance for unconventional money — gold, Bitcoin, stablecoins — also narrows, because anything that lets people exit the system reduces the inflation tax base. Everything in the next five lessons should be read with the debt arithmetic in the background.

## Concrete example

Italy's debt-to-GDP ratio sat above 130% for most of the 2010s. During the 2020-22 crisis it spiked above 150%. The European Central Bank, which technically does not exist to manage Italian fiscal policy, nonetheless engaged in a series of bond-buying programs (PEPP, then TPI in 2022) whose explicit purpose was to prevent Italian bond yields from "fragmenting" away from German yields. Translation: the ECB bought Italian debt to keep Italian borrowing costs low, because if Italian yields rose to where the market would naturally have priced them, Italy would have faced a debt crisis that would have triggered a euro crisis. This is fiscal dominance, in the European version, hiding behind technical language about "monetary policy transmission." It works for now. It keeps working as long as inflation in the eurozone stays low enough that the ECB can justify the buying. The day inflation makes the buying impossible to justify, the constraint becomes visible. The ECB knows this. The Italian Treasury knows this. The bondholders know this. Almost nobody says it out loud, because saying it out loud is the kind of thing that makes the constraint bite sooner.

## Uncomfortable question

If the United States is in slow-drift fiscal dominance — not full crisis, just a regime where the central bank can no longer credibly raise rates to whatever level inflation would require — then the 2% inflation target is a lie. Not a deliberate lie. A polite fiction maintained because admitting it would accelerate the thing it is trying to manage. So: if you genuinely believed that the realized inflation rate over the next 20 years would average 4-5% rather than 2%, what is the cheapest, lowest-effort change you could make to your finances *this week* to be less wrong if that turns out to be true? Notice that "buy Bitcoin" is one possible answer, but it is not the only one, and it may not be the best one for your specific situation. The point of the question is to make you say *something specific* you would actually do, not to recruit you to a position. Most people, when they try to answer this honestly, find that they would do nothing — which is information about what they actually believe, regardless of what they tell themselves they believe.
