## Reading

Digital money is going to be most of money within a generation. What is contested is *which kind* of digital money — and the three main answers come from three different political coalitions that genuinely do not want the same thing. This lesson is about taking each of those visions seriously enough to feel their gravitational pull, before deciding which (if any) you find defensible.

The three visions are **central bank digital currency** (CBDC), **dollar (and other fiat) stablecoins**, and **Bitcoin as money**. Almost every digital-money story you read in the news is downstream of the three-way fight between these three, even when it doesn't name them. You will understand the news much better if you can place each story on this map.

**CBDC** is digital money issued directly by a central bank to citizens and businesses, bypassing the commercial banking system that currently sits between them. It is the most state-aligned vision. The political coalition behind it includes central bankers (who get a more direct policy lever), tax authorities (who get visibility into transactions that currently move through cash and gray channels), and regulators (who get the power to enforce rules at the level of the money itself rather than at the institution level). The technical claims for CBDC — instant settlement, lower payment costs, financial inclusion for the unbanked — are real and not silly. The concerns are also real and not silly. Whoever controls issuance also controls *who can hold and spend the money at all*. A CBDC, in the strong form, gives the issuing state the technical capacity to freeze your money on individual basis without involving any intermediary, to enforce expiration dates (use it or lose it), to enforce spending categories (this money can be spent on food but not alcohol), to enforce geographic constraints (this money cannot be spent more than 50 km from your registered address), and so on. China's e-CNY has experimented with several of these features. The European digital euro has explicitly carved out individual privacy protections under €1000 — which by saying so reveals the assumption that above that threshold, surveillance is the default. The US CBDC project has been on-and-off and is currently politically frozen, but the technical work continues. Whether your country gets a CBDC and what features it ships with is going to be one of the major political fights of the late 2020s.

The honest steel-man for CBDC is this: cash is already in slow decline almost everywhere, the commercial banking system that has replaced it is *also* highly surveilled (every bank transaction is reported, every threshold trips a SAR), and the actual marginal increase in surveillance from a well-designed CBDC over the existing system may be smaller than libertarians claim. The honest case against is that the *technical capacity* a CBDC creates can be used by future governments you have not voted for, and that a system designed to do A under one administration will be used to do B under another, and the architecture is the part that gets locked in. Both claims are true at once.

**Stablecoins** are tokens issued on a public blockchain by a private entity, redeemable 1:1 for a unit of fiat currency held in reserve. The dominant ones in 2026 are USDT (Tether), USDC (Circle), and a handful of regulated bank-issued tokens that emerged after the GENIUS Act and similar legislation passed in the US in 2025. Stablecoins are not a state vision and not really a Bitcoin vision; they are a *banking-system-on-public-rails* vision. They use the public blockchain plumbing of crypto (global, programmable, 24/7, interoperable with smart contracts, settling in seconds for cents) but their issuers are subject to ordinary commercial regulation and their reserves sit in ordinary banks and Treasury bills. The political coalition is interesting and underappreciated: it includes the US Treasury (because every dollar in a stablecoin reserve buys a US Treasury bond), payment-system disruptors who don't want to wait for SWIFT, dollarized-economy users in failing-currency countries, and a chunk of the existing crypto industry that has decided stablecoins are a more practical product than Bitcoin for most everyday use cases.

The honest steel-man for stablecoins: by 2026, they move more transaction value annually than Visa or Mastercard, they have given billions of people in failing-currency economies access to dollars without needing a US bank account, and they did this with very little visible state coercion. They are arguably the most successful crypto product of the 17-year experiment, and their success embarrasses both the maximalists (the winning product is a dollar, not Bitcoin) and the regulators (the product won despite them). The honest case against: a dollar stablecoin extends the dollar's reach into jurisdictions that previously had monetary autonomy, it concentrates an enormous share of the global payment system in two or three private issuers whose reserves the US can freeze at will, and "dollar without a US bank account" still means *a dollar*, with all the soft-money properties Module 1 covered. Stablecoins are the dollar's revenge on Bitcoin: the dollar making itself into the thing Bitcoin promised to be — instant, global, digital. That this happened is the most underappreciated macro story of the decade.

**Bitcoin as money** is the vision Module 2 covered in detail. The political coalition is the most diverse and the smallest of the three: hard-money believers, civil-libertarians worried about CBDC surveillance, citizens of failed-currency economies who have lost faith in fiat alternatives, and a long tail of people across the ideological spectrum who agree on very little except that they want a monetary system that no central bank can adjust. Bitcoin is the *only* one of the three visions that takes the soft-money critique seriously enough to actually fix it at the protocol level. It is also the slowest, the least programmable, and the most volatile, and the volatility is a real obstacle to any role beyond store-of-value-on-long-horizons.

The honest steel-man for Bitcoin: in a world where the long arc of fiscal dominance from Lesson 1 plays out as the bears expect, Bitcoin's core promise (a politically un-debaseable asset) becomes precisely the thing the surviving capital wants to be denominated in, and any other answer (gold, real estate, equities, stablecoins) inherits some flavor of the same problem it is trying to escape. The honest case against: the volatility makes it useless as a unit of account on policy-relevant timescales, the 21M cap is a *promise* that depends on social consensus around the protocol holding for decades (and consensus can shift), and Bitcoin's energy footprint and political optics make it vulnerable to coordinated regulatory action in any jurisdiction whose government decides to act. Bitcoin's bet is that no such coordinated action ever quite succeeds. That bet has been right for 17 years. Past performance is not a guarantee of future results.

Now look at the three together. They are not necessarily competing for the same role. Map the use cases against the contenders and you do not get a single winner. **Stable-economy retail** stays with bank-issued credit cards or the local CBDC. **Failing-economy retail** goes to dollar stablecoins, because they offer the dollar people actually want at the speed they need without cooperation from the local government. **Long-horizon store of value, hostile to debasement** is Bitcoin's strongest case (alongside gold), because stablecoins and CBDCs inherit the underlying fiat's debasement. **Cross-border payments outside the SWIFT envelope** is mostly stablecoins today, with Bitcoin competitive on longer settlement horizons.

What you actually get in the 2030s is a *layered* system in which CBDCs handle stable-economy retail, stablecoins handle cross-border and failing-economy retail, Bitcoin handles a slice of long-horizon store-of-value alongside gold, and the existing commercial banking system handles most of the credit creation that none of the three really do well. This outcome satisfies almost nobody, because each of the three coalitions wants their preferred answer to become *dominant* rather than coexist.

The contested question is what happens at the edges. In a country whose currency is collapsing, does the state declare its CBDC mandatory and ban stablecoins? In a fiscal-dominance crisis, does the state make holding Bitcoin functionally illegal through hostile tax treatment? Does the US pressure stablecoin issuers to freeze wallets in jurisdictions it dislikes? (Yes, repeatedly, since 2022.) These are the fights that determine the shape of the system, and they are political fights more than technical ones. The technology is mostly built. What is being decided is who gets to use what for what.

## Concrete example

In 2025, Argentina's new monetary authority allowed dollar stablecoins to be used legally for ordinary commerce — something like 40% of remittances into Argentina by Q4 2025 were arriving as USDC or USDT and being cashed out (or held) directly by recipients. Domestic Bitcoin holdings also rose, but the stablecoin number dwarfed it. The Argentine peso, meanwhile, lost about another 50% of its dollar value over the year. A naive Bitcoin maximalist read of Argentina would predict Bitcoin adoption surging. The actual data showed *stablecoin adoption surging much faster*, because stablecoins solved the immediate problem (preserve purchasing power against the peso, transact today) better than Bitcoin did over a 12-month horizon. Bitcoin's role in Argentina is real but it is the *long-horizon* role for the savers who can afford volatility. Stablecoins are the live-this-month role for everyone else. Both are growing. The CBDC story in Argentina, meanwhile, is dead — nobody trusts a peso-CBDC built by the same authority that just blew up the peso.

This is what the layered three-way system actually looks like on the ground in a country where the contest is no longer hypothetical.

## Uncomfortable question

If you live in a stable-currency country and your government rolls out a CBDC in 2028, with default-on programmable features (expiration dates, category restrictions, geographic restrictions, the ability to be frozen by court order without an intermediary), would you use it for your salary? Would you use it for your savings? Would you object publicly? Would you do anything? Most people in stable-currency countries will reflexively say "I trust my government enough to use it" — and that may be true for *this* government. The harder version of the question is: do you trust the next five governments? The fifteenth? The architecture you sign up for now will outlive the politicians who built it. That is what makes the CBDC question different from any of the other digital-money questions in this lesson, and it is the question almost no public debate is willing to have honestly.
