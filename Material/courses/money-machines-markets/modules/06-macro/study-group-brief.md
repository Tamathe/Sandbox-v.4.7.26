# Module 6 — Study Group: The Country Forecast Tournament

## Format

Groups of four. Each person in the group is randomly assigned a country (drawn at the start of the session, no swapping). Over 60 minutes you will each produce a 2-minute forecast for your assigned country, listen to the other three, and then vote — not on the most optimistic forecast, but on the one that is *most likely to actually happen*. The winning forecast (group consensus) gets posted to the module discussion thread.

## The country pool

Draw one randomly from this list (use any random method — `1d10` works):

1. United States
2. Japan
3. Germany
4. China
5. Argentina
6. Nigeria
7. South Korea
8. United Kingdom
9. India
10. Saudi Arabia

These are deliberately a mix: large vs small, aging vs young, debt-stressed vs not, energy-rich vs not, dollar-dependent vs not. The point is that no two people in the group will be solving the same forecasting problem, and the cross-comparison is where the learning is.

## Structure (60 minutes total)

1. **Draw and prep (10 min)** — Each person draws a country and silently prepares a 2-minute forecast covering: (a) the country's debt and demographic situation, (b) which of the three digital-money visions dominates there in 2035, (c) how AI labor effects play out, (d) the single most plausible failure mode. No talking. No internet. Use what is in your head from the lessons.
2. **Forecasts (16 min)** — 4 people × 2 minutes of forecast + 2 minutes of clarifying questions from the group. Strict timing — set a phone timer.
3. **Cross-comparison (15 min)** — Together: which two of the four countries face the *most similar* core challenge, and which two face the *most different* core challenges? This question is harder than it looks because the surface similarities are misleading. Spend the time on it.
4. **Vote on most-likely (8 min)** — Each person silently picks the *one forecast* (not their own) they think is most likely to actually play out. Reveal simultaneously. Discuss the disagreements. The goal here is *not* unanimity — disagreement is information.
5. **Joint reflection (8 min)** — Together, write a single paragraph (4–6 sentences) answering: *what was the most surprising connection across countries that the four of us found together?* Post it to the module discussion thread tagged `#study-group-mod6`.
6. **Submit (3 min)** — Post + add the country assignments at the end of the post so other groups can compare.

## Rules

- No phones except for one timer per group.
- No looking up sources during the prep or forecast. Use what is in your head from the lessons. The point is to find out where your understanding is shallow.
- During the vote, you are *not* allowed to vote for your own forecast. The point of the structure is to force you to evaluate other people's reasoning, not to defend your own.
- "I don't know" is a valid answer to a clarifying question and is rewarded by the rubric, not punished. The dishonest answer is the made-up confident one.
- The goal is *not* to have the most optimistic or most pessimistic forecast. The goal is to have the most *defensible* one. A boring forecast that holds up under questioning beats a dramatic forecast that falls apart on the third follow-up.

## Why this format

The temptation in a module like this is to retreat into abstractions ("debt is high, demographics are bad, AI is coming"). Forcing each person to commit to *one country* with *one forecast* with *one specific failure mode* short-circuits that. You can't make a 2-minute forecast about Japan without picking a side on whether automation can substitute fast enough for the missing workers. You can't make a 2-minute forecast about Argentina without picking a side on whether stablecoins or Bitcoin take the medium-of-exchange role. The cross-comparison step then makes it visible that countries with surface similarities (Japan and Italy: both old, both indebted) actually face very different problems (Italy is in a currency union it can't print, Japan owns most of its own debt at low rates), while countries that look unrelated (Saudi Arabia and Nigeria: oil-dependent at very different stages of development) sometimes face structurally similar bets. The point is to learn macro the way it actually has to be done — country by country, with the specifics doing the work.

Tribes do not learn. Forecasting tournaments do. Bring your honest uncertainty.
