# Assignment — Send Yourself a Testnet Transaction and Explain Every Field

## The prompt

You are going to do two things:

1. **Actually do it.** Install a Bitcoin testnet wallet, get some testnet coins from a public faucet, and send them to yourself. (Testnet bitcoin has no monetary value. The faucet is free. The point is to put your hands on the system rather than read about it.)
2. **Explain it.** Write 600 words walking through the resulting transaction, field by field, in your own words. The reader of your writeup should be able to look at the same transaction in a block explorer and understand everything they see, because of what you wrote.

## What "actually do it" looks like

1. Install a wallet that supports testnet. Reasonable options include **Electrum** (set network to testnet on first launch), **Sparrow** (toggle testnet in settings), or any mobile wallet with a testnet mode. *Do not use a mainnet wallet for this assignment. Testnet coins go to testnet addresses; mainnet coins go to mainnet addresses; mixing them up is the most common mistake people make.*
2. Generate a receiving address. Note its prefix — testnet addresses start with `m`, `n`, `2`, or `tb1` depending on type, and look visibly different from mainnet ones.
3. Find a public testnet faucet. Search for one — they come and go. Paste your address; receive a small amount of testnet BTC after a captcha.
4. Once the faucet transaction confirms (one block is enough for testnet purposes), construct a new transaction sending some of those coins to a *second* address generated by your own wallet. The wallet will let you do this through normal "send" UI. Set the fee yourself rather than letting the wallet choose — you should know what you are paying and why.
5. Broadcast the transaction. Wait for it to confirm.
6. Open a public testnet block explorer (`mempool.space/testnet` or `blockstream.info/testnet`). Paste your transaction ID. You are now looking at the artifact you will write about.

## What the writeup needs to cover

Your 600 words must explain — *in your own words, not copied from a block explorer's tooltip* — what each of the following fields is and what role it plays in the protocol:

- **Transaction ID (txid)** — what it is a hash of, and why two semantically-identical transactions can have different txids.
- **Inputs**: where they come from, how the prior UTXO is referenced, what the script in the input is doing (you don't need to read script bytecode; you need to know what the script's *purpose* is).
- **Outputs**: how the value is denominated, what address format you used, what scriptPubKey is doing in plain language.
- **Fee**: what determines it, how the wallet decided how much, and what would have happened if you had set it too low.
- **Confirmations**: what the count means, why "1 confirmation" is sufficient on testnet but not on mainnet, and what the relationship is between confirmations and probabilistic finality.
- **The change output**: which output is your change, how you can tell, and what an outside observer could infer about your wallet from looking at it.

You may also include any field you found interesting or confusing. Surprises are good. If there is a field on the explorer page that you cannot explain after the lessons and a reasonable amount of looking, *say so explicitly* — that honesty is worth more than guessing.

## What I'm looking for

This is graded against the **Written Thesis Rubric** (`rubrics/written-thesis.json`). The four criteria are:

1. **Clarity of claim** — Each field's role should be stated plainly enough that a reader can summarize it back.
2. **Quality of reasoning** — When you explain *why* a field is structured the way it is (e.g., why Bitcoin uses scriptPubKey instead of a destination identifier), the reasoning should follow.
3. **Engagement with the strongest counter-argument** — In one paragraph, identify the design choice in the transaction that you find most questionable (e.g., the visibility of the change output, the size of the fee market, the lack of a "memo" field) and steel-man both the choice and the alternative.
4. **Voice and honesty** — I want to know what surprised you while doing this. What did the abstract description in the lessons turn out to mean concretely? What did you get wrong on first try?

## What will sink it

- A 600-word writeup that you could have produced *without doing the practical step*. If your writeup contains no specific reference to your actual transaction (your txid, your fee, your change address pattern, the actual block height, your real surprises), it is failing the assignment.
- An unhedged "Bitcoin's transaction model is obviously the best design" with no engagement with what could make that wrong.
- An unhedged dismissal of UTXOs ("just use accounts like Ethereum, this is silly") with no engagement with what they actually buy you.
- AI-generated prose. I will recognize it. So will you when you read it back.

## Format

- 600 words (±100)
- Plain markdown
- Include the **testnet transaction ID** at the top of the writeup so I can pull up the same transaction in an explorer while reading.
- A one-line note at the end disclosing any AI tool you used and how. ("Used Claude as a sparring partner for the design-choice paragraph." That's enough.)

## Why this assignment

The lessons spent six readings building a mental model of Bitcoin as a system. The mental model is brittle until you have *touched* the system. You can read about UTXOs forever and still be confused about what they look like in practice. Spending forty minutes putting your hands on a testnet wallet collapses the abstraction into something concrete, and then the rest of the course can build on a real foundation rather than a paraphrased one. This is the assignment most students later say was the moment Bitcoin stopped being a topic and started being a thing.

The first time you set a fee yourself and see your transaction get confirmed because you set it correctly — and the second time you set it too low and watch it sit in the mempool — the rest of Module 2's vocabulary will mean something it cannot mean from reading alone.
