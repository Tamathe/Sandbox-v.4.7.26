## Reading

Most people, asked where money came from, will tell a story that goes something like this: once upon a time everyone bartered. Bartering was annoying because of the *coincidence of wants* problem — the cobbler who wants bread has to find a baker who happens to want shoes, today, in his size. So people invented money as a clever lubricant. Coins, then paper, then cards, then phones. Progress.

It is a tidy story. It is also, as far as anthropologists can tell, basically wrong.

What the historical and ethnographic record actually shows is older and weirder. Pre-monetary societies ran mostly on **credit and obligation**, tracked socially. You gave your neighbor a fish. Your neighbor remembered. Months later, when you needed help building a roof, the neighbor showed up. Nobody wrote anything down. Nobody used coins. The "money" was the web of obligations the village kept in its collective head. David Graeber, the anthropologist, is the loudest voice making this case, and economists have fought him on the details, but the core point survives: barter as a *primary* economic mode is mostly a myth invented retroactively to make the existence of money feel inevitable.

So why did money — physical, portable, fungible money — emerge anyway? Because credit and obligation work great inside a village and badly across villages. Once you have to deal with strangers, traders, soldiers, and tax collectors, you need something that can move, that doesn't depend on remembering a face, and that the other side will accept on sight. Enter commodity money.

The early commodity monies are the ones you've heard of: cattle (the word *pecuniary* comes from *pecus*, Latin for cattle), salt (the word *salary*), cowrie shells, beads, copper, silver, gold. What they all share is some combination of: scarce, durable, divisible, recognizable, and — crucially — *hard to fake*. Different cultures landed on different objects. Yap islanders used giant stone discs. East Africans used cattle. West Africans used cowries. Eurasia eventually consolidated on metal because metal is the best compromise across all five properties.

Then comes the second jump: **representative money**. Carrying gold around is dangerous and inconvenient, so you deposit it with someone trustworthy — a goldsmith, later a bank — and they give you a paper receipt. The receipt circulates. Eventually nobody bothers to redeem it; the paper itself becomes the thing people exchange. As long as the issuer doesn't issue more receipts than they have gold, the system works. As long as.

Then the third jump, the strange one: **fiat money**. The receipts stop being backed by anything. You hold paper (or, increasingly, a database entry) that is worth what it's worth because the government says it is and because everyone else accepts it. There is no commodity behind it. The value is purely social. The United States made this jump fully in 1971, when Nixon ended the convertibility of dollars to gold. Every major economy now runs on fiat.

And then the fourth jump, which we are still in the middle of: **digital and programmable money**. Most fiat is already digital — physical cash is a small fraction of the dollar supply. But "digital" in the sense of "a database at a bank" is different from "digital" in the sense of "a token that exists natively on a network with its own rules." Bitcoin is the first object in the second category that anyone treats seriously. Stablecoins are a strange hybrid. CBDCs would be the state-issued version of the same idea. We will spend the rest of the course on this jump.

A useful concept for the rest of the course: **moneyness**. Moneyness is not binary. Things can be *more or less* money-like depending on how well they perform money's three functions (which is the next lesson). A house is somewhat money-like — it stores value — but it is terrible as a medium of exchange. A frequent-flyer mile is somewhat money-like inside a tiny domain. A US Treasury bond is extremely money-like for institutions but not for you in a coffee shop. Bitcoin sits somewhere on this spectrum, and where exactly is contested.

The other useful concept: **monetary premium**. Some things are valuable for what they are. Other things are valuable partly because people use them as money, which means a chunk of their price is *because* they are used as money, not because of any underlying utility. Gold has a monetary premium. So, arguably, do New York real estate, fine art, and Bitcoin. The monetary premium is the part of the price that would evaporate if people stopped treating the thing as a store of value. It is the most important price you can't see directly.

## Concrete example

In 1923 Germany, the price of a loaf of bread went from 250 marks at the start of the year to 200,000,000,000 marks at the end. People famously took wheelbarrows of cash to the store and were robbed of the wheelbarrow, not the cash. What happened? The currency was fiat — backed by nothing but the German state's word — and the German state printed enormous quantities of it to pay reparations and debts. The paper still said "100 marks." The bread didn't care.

Now compare: in the same period, the price of bread *in gold grams* in Germany was roughly stable. Not perfectly, but close. Gold's monetary premium and durability across regimes showed up exactly when the fiat system showed its weakest property.

This is not an argument that gold is good and fiat is bad. It is an argument that *what backs your money matters*, and that the question of what backs it is a *political* question — one a society can answer wrongly, and has, many times.

## Uncomfortable question

If money has been everything from cattle to shells to gold to paper to database entries, what's so special about the current arrangement that makes it the obvious endpoint? Or to put it another way: when you imagine the year 2075, do you actually expect the dollar (or the euro, or the yuan) to be the unit of account in the same way it is today? If not, what replaces it — and who decides?

Hold your answer. We will keep coming back to it.
