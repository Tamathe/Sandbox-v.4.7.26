## Reading

When the news says "inflation rose to 3.2% this month," what is being measured? More importantly, who chose what to measure?

Let's get a definition straight first. **Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level. The "general" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, because not all prices move together. Eggs spike. Rent climbs steadily. TVs fall every year. Healthcare goes up faster than almost everything else. Housing in coastal cities decouples from housing in the Midwest. To produce a single number called "inflation," somebody has to decide which prices to track, how to weight them, and how to handle the fact that the basket of goods a normal person buys today is different from the basket their parents bought.

That somebody is, in the United States, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the number is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**. CPI is the most-cited inflation measure in the world. It is also a *constructed* number, full of methodological choices that have changed over time. Two of the most consequential:

- **Hedonic adjustment.** If a 2026 laptop is twice as fast as a 2010 laptop at the same price, CPI will treat the 2026 laptop as *cheaper* in real terms, because you're getting more computer per dollar. This is defensible — quality has gone up. But it also means the headline inflation number can underweight the lived experience of "stuff costs more" because some of the stuff is silently being treated as having gotten better.
- **Owner's equivalent rent.** Housing is the biggest single line in most people's budgets. CPI doesn't use *house prices* directly. It uses an estimate of what homeowners *would* pay to rent their own homes. This smooths out the housing component dramatically and explains a lot of the gap between "official inflation" and "what people feel." When house prices doubled from 2019 to 2023, CPI's housing component did not double.

None of this is fraud. The methodologies are public, and there are reasons for each choice. But it does mean the headline number is a *political* number in the sense that any single-digit summary of a complicated reality is. Different countries measure differently. Different decades measure differently. If you go back and recompute 1970s inflation using current methodology, you get a much lower number. If you go and recompute current inflation using 1970s methodology, you get a much higher one. Which is "real"? Both. Neither. The question is malformed.

Now: where does inflation actually *come from*? Three overlapping mechanisms.

**Demand-pull inflation.** Too much money chasing too few goods. People want to buy more than the economy can currently produce, and prices rise to clear the market. This is what you get from stimulus checks landing in everyone's account during a supply-constrained period — exactly what happened in 2020–2022.

**Cost-push inflation.** Inputs get more expensive. A war disrupts oil supply, energy prices spike, and the cost of producing almost everything else rises in turn. Cost-push inflation looks like demand-pull on the surface — prices going up — but the mechanism is different and it requires different responses.

**Monetary inflation.** The supply of money grows faster than the supply of goods. This is the "too much money" half of demand-pull, made explicit. The classical monetarist view, associated with Milton Friedman, is that inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." Modern macro mostly agrees that monetary expansion *can* drive inflation, but disagrees about how mechanical the link is and over what time horizon.

The reason it matters which kind of inflation you're looking at is that they call for different policy responses. Demand-pull responds to higher interest rates (cool the demand). Cost-push doesn't really, because tightening monetary policy doesn't make oil cheaper. Monetary inflation responds to slowing money creation. Mistaking one for another — say, raising rates aggressively to fight a cost-push shock — causes recession without solving the inflation, because the diagnosis was wrong.

Now the harder claim, which is the title of this lesson: **inflation is a policy choice**.

The standard objection is "no it isn't, supply shocks happen, wars happen, pandemics happen — those aren't choices." That's true, and it's why this claim needs to be stated carefully. The point is not that policymakers choose every price tick. The point is that policymakers choose:

- How fast the money supply grows (through interest rates, QE, and fiscal stimulus)
- How tightly to fight inflation when it appears (through the same tools, in reverse)
- How much "tolerable" inflation to define as the target (the Fed's official target is 2%, which is a *choice*, not a law of physics — and it's a target they have explicitly said they'll allow to overshoot for periods of time)
- How to *measure* inflation (through CPI methodology)

Each of those is a decision made by a small number of people in offices in Washington and Frankfurt and Tokyo, and the decisions could be otherwise. A 0% inflation target is policy-feasible. A 4% target is policy-feasible. A "we will print whatever it takes to fund the government and let inflation run hot" target is policy-feasible — and is what several countries have effectively chosen at various points.

The reason most central banks land on something like 2% rather than 0% is interesting and worth sitting with. The official reason is that 0% is too close to deflation, and deflation is dangerous because it makes debts harder to repay (you owe in dollars that are getting more valuable). But notice how that reasoning circles back: the system is biased toward expansion, debts are large, deflation hurts debtors, and the largest debtor in the world is the government itself. A small steady positive inflation rate quietly transfers value from creditors to debtors. The biggest debtor benefits. This is not a conspiracy, it is just an incentive, and incentives compound.

This is where the Bitcoin argument enters the conversation, and why we are talking about all of this in a course that is supposedly about Bitcoin and AI. The Bitcoin claim is not "inflation is bad." The Bitcoin claim is: **money should not be a policy variable in the first place**. The supply should be set by rules, not by committees, because committees are subject to political pressures the rules are not. Whether you find that compelling depends on whether you trust committees more than rules. Reasonable people, again, disagree.

There is also a real counter-argument. A money supply that *can't* respond to crises is brittle. The 2008 financial crisis would have been much, much worse without central banks aggressively expanding credit. The 2020 pandemic shutdown would have caused mass bankruptcies. A pure rule-based money has no answer to these moments. The answer "it shouldn't" is a coherent answer, but it has costs that fall on real people. The honest version of the debate is between *flexibility with discretion-creep risk* and *predictability with crisis-response brittleness*. Both sides have a point. Anyone who tells you only one side does is selling.

## Concrete example

Between February 2020 and March 2022, the US M2 money supply grew by about 40%. That is a staggering number — historically, post-war US M2 had grown around 6% per year. In two years, M2 grew by what would normally take six or seven. Some of this expansion was the COVID emergency response. Some of it was the Fed's QE programs. Some of it was direct stimulus payments to households.

Inflation followed, peaking at about 9% (CPI) in mid-2022. A lot of economists, including ones who had been confident hyperinflation wouldn't happen after 2008, were caught off guard. Why was 2020 different from 2008? Because in 2008 the new money sat in bank reserves and didn't enter the broader economy. In 2020 it bypassed banks and was put into people's bank accounts directly. Velocity didn't get a chance to fail.

This is the strongest piece of evidence in modern memory that a deliberate policy choice — to flood the system with new money via fiscal stimulus — produced exactly the inflation a strict monetarist would have predicted. Not perfectly. Not on a clean schedule. But it happened, and it was a *choice*, and the choice could have been made differently.

## Uncomfortable question

The Fed targets 2% inflation. Compounded over 30 years, 2% inflation reduces the purchasing power of a dollar by about 45%. If you save $1 in 2026 and hold it as a dollar through 2056, and the Fed hits its target every year, you will have about 55 cents of 2026 purchasing power left. **Why is 2% the right target, rather than 0%?** Try to give the strongest argument for it without using the word "deflation" as a magic word. Then give the strongest argument against it. Notice which argument felt more familiar to you, and ask yourself why.
