# Capstone: A Change Management Plan with ROI and Ethics

## The scenario

Pick a real or hypothetical EHR-related initiative at your institution that involves a significant change in clinician behavior, workflow, or content. Examples that work well: deploying a new CDS rule for sepsis or anticoagulation; rolling out a new documentation template; transitioning from one order set library to another; deploying an AI-driven clinical tool; implementing a new bedside medication administration workflow; replacing a major sub-system within the EHR. The initiative should be substantial enough to support the full analysis below — not a typo fix, not a single-screen redesign.

Write a **1,200–1,600 word change management plan** that includes a defended choice of change-management framework, a governance structure, a rough ROI case, and an explicit treatment of the ethics of clinician burden created by the change.

## What the plan must do

1. **Describe the initiative.** Three to five sentences. What is being changed, who it affects, what the intended benefit is, what the rough scope and time horizon are. Abstract any institutional details that would compromise confidentiality.

2. **Pick and defend a named change-management framework — Kotter, ADKAR, or Lewin.** Do not pick all three. Pick one. Explain why it is the best fit for this specific initiative based on the scope, the time horizon, and the locus of the change (institutional vs. individual). The grading will reward a defended specific choice over a hedged "we will use elements of all three." If your initiative genuinely needs more than one (e.g., Kotter for leadership and ADKAR for individual diagnosis), say so explicitly and justify the combination.

3. **Apply the chosen framework concretely.** Walk through the framework's stages or steps and say what you would actually do at each one. For Kotter, what is the urgency message and who is in the guiding coalition? For ADKAR, what is your plan for each of the five letters and where do you expect the failures to occur? For Lewin, what is the unfreeze move, what is the change move, and — critically — what is the refreeze plan?

4. **Name the governance structure.** Which committees own which decisions? Who is the single Accountable for the initiative? What is the RACI for the recurring decision classes the initiative will require (build, test, deploy, monitor, retire)? Be specific. "The CDS Committee will be involved" is not specific enough.

5. **Build a rough ROI case.** Use the discipline from lesson 3. Identify hard benefits and soft benefits and label each. Identify direct costs and avoided costs. Estimate the full TCO over a defensible time horizon (not just the line-item license cost). Present a range, not a point estimate. Do at least one sensitivity analysis showing how the conclusion changes when a key assumption changes. Be explicit about what you are guessing and what you are documenting.

6. **Address the ethics of clinician burden.** The change will impose some cost on the clinicians who have to adopt it — additional clicks, additional cognitive load, additional friction, additional time. Acknowledge the cost. Explain how you will design the change to minimize it. Identify the workflow patterns from Module 5 that the change is at risk of disrupting. Treat resistance as data, not as a character flaw.

7. **Apply Friedman's fundamental theorem as the closing frame.** State, explicitly, what the predefined criteria are for declaring the intervention a success or a failure at a six-month or twelve-month review. State what you would do if the intervention does not meet the success criteria — including the possibility of retraction. The discipline of writing this down before launch is the closing move of the course.

8. **Engage the strongest counter-argument.** The strongest counter-argument is some version of: "the change-management plan is well-intentioned but the institution does not have the operational discipline to execute it; the ROI case rests on optimistic assumptions; the ethics framing is sincere but does not change the fact that the clinicians on the receiving end will experience the change as burden regardless of how well it is designed; the predefined retraction criteria will not be enforced when the moment comes." Steel-man this. Then respond.

## What the plan must NOT do

- Identify any specific patient. See the PHI Handling Policy.
- Identify a specific institution by name in a way that would compromise confidentiality.
- Pick a change-management framework based on which one you remember best. The grading rewards a defended specific choice over a default.
- Hide the soft benefits and the optimistic assumptions in the ROI case. Honesty is the criterion, not optimism.
- Treat clinician resistance as a character flaw or as a problem to be overcome through discipline. Resistance is design feedback.
- Be drafted by an AI. See the AI Use Policy.

## How it will be graded

By the **Written Analysis Rubric**. The "Use of frameworks and vocabulary" criterion is the heaviest hitter — graders are looking for the change-management framework, the RACI, the TCO, the hard-vs-soft benefit distinction, and Friedman's corollary doing real work, not a paragraph that names them and moves on. The "Engagement with the strongest counter-argument" criterion will weigh against you if you treat the plan as obviously correct; the strongest plan acknowledges that change management in healthcare is structurally hard and that even the best-designed plans fail more often than they succeed.

## A note on what this assignment is for

The assignment is the integral of the course. It asks you to use the project-management vocabulary from lesson 1, the governance discipline from lesson 2, the ROI honesty from lesson 3, and the change-management and ethics framing from lesson 4 — all on the same initiative, in a single document, defended end-to-end. If you can write this plan well, you have internalized the operational and ethical machinery the course was built to teach. The boards test the recognition of the frameworks. The job tests whether you can use them all at once on something the institution will actually fund. The capstone is the rehearsal for the second.
