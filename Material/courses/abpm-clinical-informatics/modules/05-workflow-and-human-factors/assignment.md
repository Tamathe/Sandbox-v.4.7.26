# Capstone: A Sociotechnical Failure Analysis

## The scenario

Pick a real or published EHR-related failure, near-miss, or persistent problem you know well enough to describe. It can be from your own experience, from a published case (JAMIA, Applied Clinical Informatics, *Wachter's The Digital Doctor*, ECRI Institute reports, ONC SAFER case studies), or from an institutional incident report (de-identified). The failure should be substantial enough to support analysis through the Sittig-Singh model — not a single typo, but something with multiple contributors.

Write a **1,200–1,600 word sociotechnical failure analysis** that walks the failure through the Sittig-Singh eight-dimension model and proposes a multi-dimensional fix.

## What the analysis must do

1. **Describe the failure precisely.** What happened, to whom, in what setting, with what outcome? Three to five sentences. Abstract any PHI per the policy from Module 1.

2. **Walk all eight Sittig-Singh dimensions.** For each dimension, state whether it contributed to the failure and how. Some dimensions will not have contributed; say so explicitly. Do not skip a dimension just because it is "obviously irrelevant" — the discipline of considering all eight is the point of the exercise.

3. **Identify the primary contributors and the secondary contributors.** Most real failures have one or two dimensions that are primary and several that are contributing. Be honest about which is which.

4. **Apply the Five Rights from Module 4** to any CDS-related component of the failure, if applicable. Not all sociotechnical failures involve CDS, but most CDS failures are sociotechnical, and the two frameworks reinforce each other.

5. **Map the failure to a SAFER Guide** and identify which specific recommendation, if implemented, would have prevented or mitigated it. If no SAFER recommendation maps cleanly, say so and explain what gap that reveals.

6. **Propose a multi-dimensional fix.** For each primary contributing dimension, propose a specific fix. The fix should be one that the institution could actually implement — not a wish list. Identify the trade-off you are accepting with each fix.

7. **Specify the monitoring (dimension 8) that would catch the next instance earlier.** If the failure had been detected earlier, what specifically would have produced the alert? Who would have read it? What would have triggered the response?

8. **Engage the strongest counter-argument.** The strongest counter-argument is some version of "the team that built this had reasons that were defensible at the time, and your fix introduces its own risks." Steel-man it. Then respond.

## What the analysis must NOT do

- Identify any specific patient. Abstract before you write. See the PHI Handling Policy.
- Identify a specific institution by name in a way that would compromise confidentiality.
- Blame an individual clinician. Sociotechnical analysis is structural; if your fix is "the resident should have been more careful," you have not done the analysis.
- Skip dimensions that seem irrelevant. The discipline is the point.
- Be drafted by an AI. See the AI Use Policy.

## How it will be graded

By the **Written Analysis Rubric**. The "Use of frameworks and vocabulary" criterion is the heaviest hitter for this assignment — graders are looking for the eight-dimension walk doing real work, not a paragraph with the dimensions listed and ignored. The "Engagement with the strongest counter-argument" criterion will weigh against you if you treat the team that built the failed system as people who should have known better; the strongest analysis acknowledges that the failure was foreseeable in retrospect and not in prospect.

## Submission

Paste the analysis as a single discussion post in the Module 5 capstone thread. The instructor will review against the rubric within five days. You may revise once.

## A note on what this assignment is for

This is the assignment that turns the field's frameworks into an actual analytical practice. The Sittig-Singh model is the most-tested and most-applied framework on the boards, and the discipline of walking all eight dimensions before proposing a fix is the discipline that distinguishes a competent informaticist from a board-passing one. The exam item writers do not give you eight-dimension stems explicitly, but the right answers to many of their questions implicitly require the multi-dimensional view. Practicing it on a real case is how that view becomes habit.
