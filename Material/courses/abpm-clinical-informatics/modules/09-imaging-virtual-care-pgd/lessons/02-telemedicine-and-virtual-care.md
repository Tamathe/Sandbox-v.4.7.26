## Reading

Telemedicine sits in an awkward place on the boards. It is operationally unglamorous — the standards are thinner than HL7 or DICOM, the technology is mostly off-the-shelf video, and most of the hard problems are regulatory rather than informatic. But the boards test it anyway, because the regulatory layer is precisely where a CMIO is expected to have an opinion and because telemedicine policy changed more between 2020 and 2025 than in the previous twenty years combined. You should be able to walk a stem through the four modalities, the DEA controlled-substance rules, the licensure problem, the parity laws, the technical requirements, and the failure modes — in that order — without hesitation.

Start with the **four modalities**. They are not interchangeable, and the boards write stems where the wrong modality is the wrong answer.

- **Synchronous video** is real-time two-way audio-video between clinician and patient. The default mental model most people have for "telemedicine," but only one of four. Useful when the clinical encounter requires live interaction (a urgent care visit, a behavioral health session, a follow-up). Requires bandwidth on both sides, a HIPAA-compliant video platform, and a clinician licensed in the state the patient is physically located in at the time of the visit.
- **Store-and-forward** (asynchronous) is the transmission of clinical information — typically images, but also videos, ECG traces, or structured questionnaires — from a patient or referring clinician to a specialist who reviews them on their own time and replies. Teledermatology was the first widely deployed example: a primary care clinician photographs a lesion, uploads it with a brief history, and a dermatologist reviews and recommends within 24–72 hours. Teleradiology, telepathology, and teleophthalmology (diabetic retinopathy screening with later grader review) are all store-and-forward. Store-and-forward unlocks specialist capacity in ways synchronous video cannot: one dermatologist can handle hundreds of store-and-forward consults per week from rural primary care.
- **Remote patient monitoring (RPM)** is the asynchronous collection of patient physiologic data from devices in the patient's home — blood pressure cuffs, glucose meters, weight scales, pulse oximeters, continuous glucose monitors, implantable cardiac devices — transmitted to a clinician for ongoing review. RPM lives in its own CPT family (covered in the next lesson) and is the modality most economically aligned with value-based care.
- **E-consult** is a clinician-to-clinician asynchronous specialist consultation, usually inside the EHR's messaging or referral system. The PCP writes a structured question with relevant data; the specialist replies with a recommendation; no patient encounter occurs. E-consult is the most underused modality in the United States and the one that consistently reduces unnecessary specialty referrals when it is paid for. Project ECHO is the named program the boards reuse for hub-and-spoke specialist tele-mentoring of rural primary care, which is a related but distinct pattern (ECHO is education, not consultation).

The boards reward you for picking the right modality on a stem. A skin lesion that does not need real-time interaction is store-and-forward, not video. A diabetic with hypertension being managed at home is RPM. A primary care clinician asking a cardiologist whether a borderline ECG needs further workup is e-consult. An urgent behavioral health visit is synchronous video.

Now the **regulatory layer**, which is where most of the test items live.

**The DEA Ryan Haight Online Pharmacy Consumer Protection Act of 2008** is the federal statute that, until 2020, required at least one in-person evaluation by a DEA-registered prescriber before any controlled substance could be prescribed via telemedicine. The Act was written in response to internet pharmacies prescribing controlled substances without any clinical relationship at all, and its in-person requirement was intentional. Ryan Haight permits exceptions — including the "practice of telemedicine" exception that requires a special DEA telemedicine registration that DEA never actually stood up — and during the COVID-19 public health emergency the Department of Health and Human Services and DEA issued flexibilities that waived the in-person requirement and allowed Schedule II–V controlled substance prescribing via telemedicine without a prior in-person visit. Those flexibilities were extended multiple times after the PHE ended in May 2023, and DEA has since proposed (and partially implemented) a more permanent regulatory framework that retains some telemedicine prescribing for controlled substances under defined conditions. The boards expect you to know: (1) the statute by name (Ryan Haight), (2) the in-person requirement and what it was for, (3) that COVID flexibilities waived it, (4) that the long-term regulatory landscape is still in flux, and (5) that buprenorphine for opioid use disorder has been treated more permissively than other controlled substances because of the public health stakes — DEA finalized a permanent rule allowing initial buprenorphine prescribing via telemedicine without a prior in-person visit. If a stem describes "controlled substance prescribing via telemedicine," reach for Ryan Haight first.

**State licensure**. The single biggest structural friction in U.S. telemedicine is that physicians are licensed by state, not federally, and to practice medicine via telemedicine you must be licensed in the state where the *patient* is physically located at the time of the encounter, not the state where the clinician sits. A neurologist in Boston who wants to follow her patient who moved to Florida must be licensed in Florida. The cost and administrative burden of maintaining licenses in multiple states is the reason most telemedicine programs are geographically narrow.

The **Interstate Medical Licensure Compact (IMLC)** is the multi-state response: a compact (now adopted by roughly 40 states) that creates an expedited pathway for physicians who hold a full unrestricted license in their state of principal license to obtain licenses in other compact states, without going through each state's full application from scratch. IMLC does not create a single national license — it streamlines the process of obtaining multiple state licenses for physicians who qualify. Other compacts exist for nursing (NLC), psychology (PSYPACT), physical therapy (PT Compact), and others. You should know the IMLC by name and know that it is an expedited multi-state pathway, not a federal license.

**Telehealth parity laws** are state laws (and some federal Medicare provisions) that require payers to reimburse telehealth services at parity with in-person services — either coverage parity (the service must be a covered benefit if delivered via telehealth) or payment parity (the payment rate must equal the in-person rate). Parity is a state-by-state patchwork. The most-tested distinction is between *coverage* parity and *payment* parity: a state can mandate one without the other, and the absence of payment parity often determines whether a telemedicine program is financially sustainable. Medicare expanded telehealth coverage dramatically during the COVID PHE; many of those flexibilities have been extended through legislation but the long-term Medicare telehealth payment landscape remains a moving target.

**Originating site rules** are the historical Medicare requirement that a beneficiary must be physically located at a qualified "originating site" — typically a rural health clinic, hospital, or physician office in a Health Professional Shortage Area — to receive Medicare-covered telehealth. The originating site rules effectively excluded the patient's home and most urban areas from Medicare telehealth before 2020. The COVID PHE waived the originating site restrictions and allowed the home as an originating site; subsequent legislation has extended those waivers. The boards test whether you can name the originating site concept, explain what it historically restricted, and recognize that the COVID era changed it.

Now the **technical requirements**. A compliant telemedicine deployment needs (1) a HIPAA-compliant video platform with end-to-end or transport encryption, with the vendor signed to a Business Associate Agreement; (2) authentication of both clinician and patient identities; (3) audit logging consistent with the HIPAA Security Rule; (4) integration with the EHR for documentation, ordering, and billing; and (5) a downtime plan for when the video fails (a fallback to telephone, or a rescheduled visit). During the COVID PHE, HHS Office for Civil Rights announced enforcement discretion that permitted the use of non-public-facing consumer video platforms (FaceTime, Skype, Zoom in its consumer form) without a BAA — that enforcement discretion has since ended, and a permanent telemedicine deployment must be on a BAA-covered platform. The boards reuse the BAA-and-platform fact pattern.

Finally, the **failure modes**. The recurring patterns in real telemedicine programs the boards expect you to recognize:

- **The digital divide and equity gap.** Patients who lack reliable broadband, a smartphone with adequate camera and bandwidth, or the digital literacy to navigate a telemedicine platform are systematically excluded from telemedicine — and those patients overlap with the populations that have the worst health outcomes. Studies during the COVID PHE consistently showed lower telemedicine uptake among older, lower-income, rural, and non-English-speaking patients, and *higher* completion rates for telephone-only visits when video was the only alternative. A telemedicine program that does not have a telephone fallback path produces an equity gap by design. The boards write stems where the right answer is "the program excluded the population it most needed to serve."
- **Clinical scope mismatch.** Telemedicine works for some conditions and not others. A virtual urgent care visit is appropriate for a urinary tract infection in a young patient with classic symptoms; it is not appropriate for chest pain. The clinical workflow must triage which complaints are eligible for virtual care and which require in-person evaluation, and the failure mode is silent overuse of telemedicine for complaints that warranted an in-person exam.
- **Documentation and billing integration failures.** A telemedicine visit that does not capture the right place-of-service code, modality modifier, and originating site information may be denied or recouped. The boards do not test the specific modifier codes but they do test whether you understand that telemedicine billing has its own data requirements and that integration with the EHR's billing pathway is the difference between a sustainable program and an unfunded one.
- **Cross-state licensure violations.** A clinician who treats a patient who has temporarily traveled to a state where the clinician is not licensed has, technically, practiced unlicensed medicine in that state. Most programs handle this with a workflow check at the start of every encounter ("where are you physically located right now?"), and the failure mode is the program that does not.

If you can walk a stem through modality → DEA Ryan Haight if controlled substances are involved → state licensure and IMLC → parity and originating site → BAA and platform → equity and triage failure modes, you have the entire telemedicine question space the boards reuse.

## Concrete example

A regional health system in upstate New York that grew during the COVID PHE to 40% of its primary care visits being virtual is asked by its CFO to defend the program at a post-PHE leadership review. The CMIO opens with the modality breakdown: 70% synchronous video (mostly behavioral health and chronic disease follow-up), 15% store-and-forward (teledermatology and a small teleophthalmology diabetic retinopathy screening pilot), 10% e-consult (a cardiology and endocrinology hub-and-spoke pattern with the affiliated FQHCs), and 5% RPM (a hypertension and heart failure pilot). The CMIO names that the platform is on a BAA, the OCR enforcement discretion that allowed the consumer Zoom workaround in 2020 has ended and the program migrated, and that the program now restricts controlled substance prescribing for new patients to in-person visits except for buprenorphine for opioid use disorder, which DEA's permanent rule allows.

The CFO pushes on three things. First, the geography: the system has clinicians who joined during the PHE and now follow patients who have moved to Florida and Arizona, and the cross-state licensure exposure is real. The CMIO commits to enrolling those clinicians in the IMLC and to a workflow check at the start of every visit confirming the patient's physical location. Second, the parity question: New York has coverage parity but commercial payment parity is not guaranteed and Medicare's long-term telehealth payment is uncertain. The CMIO concedes that the financial sustainability depends on parity policy that is outside the system's control, and that the program has been built to be operationally efficient enough to survive at 80% parity if necessary. Third, equity: the CFO points out that the program's video uptake is lowest in the zip codes with the most uninsured patients. The CMIO commits to a telephone fallback path with documented clinical equivalence for the visit types where it is appropriate, and to a patient-experience workflow that does not require app installation for the first visit.

Nothing in this conversation is exotic. Every problem has been on the boards for a decade. The exam reuses each piece — modality choice, Ryan Haight, IMLC, parity, BAA, equity gap — as a one-line stem and expects you to name the right concept.

## Uncomfortable question

If telemedicine, after twenty years of pilots and a once-in-a-century natural experiment, still excludes the patients with the worst health outcomes more than it reaches them — and if the regulatory and payment scaffolding remains state-by-state and politically contingent — is the modern enthusiasm for "virtual-first" care a structural improvement, or is it a way to shift the cost of access onto patients who can already navigate the system? Where would you take your stand if a CFO asked you to defend a 70% virtual primary care strategy?
