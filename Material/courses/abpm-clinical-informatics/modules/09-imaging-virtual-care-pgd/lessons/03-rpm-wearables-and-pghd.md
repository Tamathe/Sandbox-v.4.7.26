## Reading

Remote patient monitoring (RPM) is the modality where the economics, the regulation, and the data quality problems all collide. The clinical case for monitoring patients between visits is a hundred years old; the reason it has finally taken off in the last decade is that CMS published a CPT code family that pays for it, the consumer device industry has produced cheap connected hardware, and value-based payment has made avoiding hospitalizations a positive line item rather than a charity case. The boards expect you to know the economic story, the data quality story, and the regulatory story — in that order.

Start with the **CPT code family**. RPM in the U.S. is paid through a small set of CPT codes published by CMS, and you do not need to memorize the specific numbers — but you should be able to name them as a family and explain the economic logic of each. The four canonical RPM codes are:

- **99453** — the *one-time setup and patient education* code. Paid once per episode of monitoring when the device is given to the patient and the patient is trained.
- **99454** — the *device supply and data transmission* code, paid every 30 days for the supply of the device and the transmission of physiologic data, conditional on at least 16 days of readings in the 30-day period. The 16-day threshold is a real and tested compliance requirement.
- **99457** — the *first 20 minutes per calendar month* of clinical staff time spent reviewing data, communicating with the patient, and managing care. Requires at least one interactive communication with the patient (typically a phone call or video) during the month.
- **99458** — *each additional 20 minutes per month* of clinical staff time, billable after 99457.

The boards do not test the four-digit codes themselves. They test whether you understand the *family*: a setup code, a recurring device-supply code with a minimum-readings threshold, a base management-time code with an interaction requirement, and add-on time codes. The same logic applies to the parallel chronic care management (CCM) and remote therapeutic monitoring (RTM) families. If a stem describes "billing for an RPM program," the answer is almost always either the 16-day threshold (the most common compliance failure) or the interactive communication requirement (the second most common).

The **economic logic** matters more than the codes. Under fee-for-service, RPM creates a small per-patient-per-month revenue stream — typically $100–150 per attributed patient per month at the time of writing — that funds the clinical staff time required to actually look at the data. That funding is what makes RPM operationally viable as a *program*, rather than as a charitable activity that sits on top of an already-overstretched clinical workflow. Under value-based contracts (capitation, full-risk Medicare Advantage, ACO with downside risk), the RPM CPT revenue is secondary to the avoided utilization the program produces — every prevented heart failure exacerbation or hypertensive crisis goes directly to the bottom line. The boards reward you for matching the payment environment to the program design (cross-reference Module 8's healthcare financing lesson). Under FFS-only, RPM lives or dies on the CPT family. Under VBC, the CPT family is a useful subsidy on top of a much larger savings story.

Now **patient-generated health data (PGHD)**. PGHD is a defined term in U.S. health policy: "health-related data created, recorded, or gathered by or from patients (or family members or other caregivers) to help address a health concern." The ONC defines it more precisely in its PGHD policy framework, and the boards expect you to recognize the term and to understand what is structurally hard about it. PGHD includes the data that flows from RPM devices, but it also includes patient-reported outcomes (PROs), patient-reported symptoms, smartphone-collected step counts and sleep data, and any free-text or structured information the patient enters into a portal or app.

The two governing problems are **provenance** and **attestation**, not volume.

*Provenance* is the question of where a data point came from. A blood pressure reading in the EHR that was measured by a medical assistant at the start of a clinic visit, with a calibrated arm cuff and a documented technique, is not the same kind of data as a blood pressure reading the patient transmitted from a home cuff of unknown brand at an unknown time of day in an unknown body position. Both are "blood pressure" in the same FHIR Observation resource, but they should not be acted on the same way. Real PGHD integration requires the EHR to carry the *source* of every reading and the clinician to be able to see it. FHIR has the `device` and `performer` reference fields exactly for this purpose; whether the EHR surfaces them in the chart is a separate question. A common failure mode is PGHD streaming into the chart with no visible distinction from clinic-measured data — and clinicians making decisions on home readings as if they were calibrated.

*Attestation* is the related problem of who can vouch for the reading. If the patient transmits a reading, can you tell whether the patient took it themselves, whether they took it correctly, and whether the device was actually on the patient (rather than on a curious child or a sleeping cat)? PGHD has no equivalent of a clinic visit's chain of custody. The honest answer is that for most PGHD streams the answer is "no, you cannot tell," and the program design must accommodate that uncertainty rather than pretending it does not exist. The boards write stems where a clinician acted on a home reading as if it were a clinic measurement and the right answer is "the program lacked a provenance/attestation framework." PGHD is not a data quality problem you solve with more data. It is a data *trust* problem you solve with governance.

**Wearables and consumer device data quality** is a related but distinct topic. The signal quality of consumer wearables varies enormously: a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) like the Dexcom or Libre is an FDA-cleared medical device with rigorously validated accuracy, while a smartwatch step count may be off by 30%. A smartwatch ECG (Apple Watch, Fitwatch derivatives) is FDA-cleared for atrial fibrillation detection and has been validated against gold-standard ECG, but the lead-I single-channel signal is not equivalent to a 12-lead ECG and was never intended to be. The clinical use case must match the device. The boards reward you for knowing that "wearable data" is a category, not a quality claim, and that the question is always "what device, validated for what indication?"

The **FDA general wellness vs medical device** line is the regulatory boundary. The FDA's 2016 General Wellness Policy guidance states that products intended for general wellness — promoting healthy lifestyle, weight management, fitness, sleep — that present a low risk to user safety are not regulated as medical devices. A step counter that encourages walking is general wellness. A continuous glucose monitor that informs insulin dosing is a medical device subject to FDA clearance. A smartwatch app that tells the user "you may be in atrial fibrillation, see a doctor" lives in a gray zone the FDA has clarified through specific Class II clearances (the Apple Watch ECG feature has its own 510(k)). The principle: *intended use* and *risk to the user* together determine regulatory status. The boards test whether you can identify which side of the line a described product sits on.

**PGHD–EHR integration patterns**. The dominant pattern in 2024–2026 is FHIR-based ingestion through patient-facing APIs (the topic of the next lesson) or through device-vendor cloud-to-cloud integrations that land in dedicated FHIR Observation resources tagged with provenance metadata. CGMs typically integrate through vendor cloud APIs (Dexcom Clarity, Libre LibreView) that push to the EHR's diabetes module. Implantable cardiac devices (pacemakers, defibrillators, loop recorders) integrate through manufacturer-specific networks (Medtronic CareLink, Boston Scientific Latitude, Abbott Merlin) that have existed since long before FHIR and that the boards still expect you to be able to name. The EHR must reconcile readings against the patient's identity — the matching problem is the same one Module 3 covers — and must surface the data in a clinical workflow that supports action.

**Alarm and alert fatigue** is the failure mode RPM produces at scale. A continuous physiologic data stream generates a continuous opportunity for alerts, and the program that fires every alert is the program that trains its clinicians to ignore them. The cross-reference is to Module 4 (alert fatigue in CDS) and Module 5 (the sociotechnical model). RPM alert design is the same problem as inpatient cardiac telemetry alarm design: the right answer is to suppress the alerts that do not change clinical action and to escalate the ones that do, with explicit thresholds that are governed and revisited. A program that generates 300 alerts per nurse per shift and acts on three of them is failing in the same way an EHR with 100 best-practice advisories per encounter is failing — both produce a workforce that has learned to dismiss the system.

A note on **equity in RPM**. The same digital divide that constrains telemedicine constrains RPM. RPM requires a patient with a working smartphone or cellular-enabled device, the digital literacy to charge and use it, and a home environment stable enough to support the program. Programs that do not account for this exclude the patients with the most to gain — and the boards write equity stems that reward you for naming the structural exclusion rather than blaming the patient.

If you can walk through the CPT family, the economic logic, the PGHD provenance/attestation problem, the FDA general wellness line, the integration patterns, alarm fatigue, and equity, you have the entire RPM/PGHD board surface.

## Concrete example

A 200-bed community hospital launches a heart failure RPM program targeting 400 attributed Medicare Advantage patients with prior heart failure admissions. The vendor provides Bluetooth-enabled scales and blood pressure cuffs that pair with a cellular hub the patient keeps next to the bed. Daily weight and blood pressure stream to the vendor's cloud, then to the EHR through a FHIR Observation feed tagged with `device` and `performer` references that identify the readings as patient-collected.

The medical director designs the alert governance up front. A weight increase of more than two pounds in 24 hours or five pounds in a week, in a patient with documented heart failure, fires a nurse-review alert. A systolic blood pressure above 180 or below 90 fires a same-day nurse contact. Everything else is reviewed in batch by the program nurse during the once-per-month interactive call required for 99457. The 16-day threshold for 99454 is enforced by a vendor-side report that flags any patient at risk of falling under the threshold by day 20 of the month, so the program can re-engage them before the billing window closes.

Six months in, the program is producing a 22% relative reduction in heart failure readmissions in the enrolled cohort and a positive contribution margin under the MA contract — the avoided admissions matter much more than the CPT revenue, but the CPT revenue covers the nurse staffing cost so the program is self-sustaining at the operational level. The two recurring problems are (1) the lowest-income zip codes have the highest enrollment refusals, with patients citing concerns about device theft and electricity costs, which the program partially addresses by providing the cellular hub at no cost and a $10/month utility subsidy; and (2) about 8% of weight readings are clearly artifactual (sudden jumps of 20+ pounds, then back to baseline the next day) and the program builds a simple plausibility filter that holds those readings for nurse confirmation rather than firing alerts. Both problems are predictable from the lecture material; both are addressable with workflow rather than technology.
