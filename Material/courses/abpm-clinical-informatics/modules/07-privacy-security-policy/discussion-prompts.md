# Module 7 Discussion Prompts

Three threads. Pick at least one and post once. Reply to at least one peer on a different thread. Read the **Case Discussion Rubric** before posting — PHI hygiene is graded.

Reminder: cases are abstracted. "A 60-year-old admitted from clinic" is fine. "The patient I admitted last Tuesday" is on its way to being PHI. The PHI Handling Policy from Module 1 applies to every post in these threads.

---

## Thread 1 — Information blocking, or legitimate clinical judgment?

Pick a real or hypothetical scenario in which a clinician (or an institution) restricted data sharing for a reason the clinician considered legitimate. The scenario should be specific enough to be analyzed: a particular type of result, a particular kind of patient, a particular workflow constraint. Describe it (abstracted).

Then argue both sides under the information blocking rule. Which of the eight exceptions, if any, would the clinician's practice plausibly fall under? What are the specific conditions of that exception, and does the practice meet them? Steel-man the case that the practice is information blocking. Steel-man the case that it qualifies for the exception. Take a side at the end.

The point of the thread is to make you exercise the eight exceptions on a real case rather than reciting them as a list. The boards reward you for naming the exception by name and applying its specific conditions. The job rewards you for the discernment to tell the difference between a clinician with a real harm concern and a clinician who is uncomfortable with the new default and reaching for the Preventing Harm exception to restore the old workflow. Both are real. They look identical from outside.

The standard wrong move on this thread is to declare immediately that the practice is or is not information blocking without engaging with the specific conditions. The grading rewards the engagement with the conditions, not the conclusion.

## Thread 2 — The HIPAA-vs-Cures tension

HIPAA's default is restriction. The Cures Act information blocking rule's default is access. The two regimes are designed to be complementary — compliance with HIPAA is not information blocking under the Privacy Exception — but in practice they pull in different directions and produce real tension at the operational level.

In this thread, defend or attack the following claim:

> "The Cures Act information blocking rule has correctly identified that the historical patterns of restriction in U.S. healthcare were rooted in market self-interest rather than in clinical or privacy concerns, and that the cultural transition from 'default no' to 'default yes' is the work of the field for the next decade. The institutions that resist the transition are not protecting patients — they are protecting institutional habits that no longer have a legitimate justification."

Take a side. Then steel-man the other side in a second paragraph. The grading is on the steel-man, not on which side you picked. The point is to surface the gap between *the regulatory direction* and *the operational reality*, which is one of the genuine fights happening in the field right now.

If you have personal experience on either side — having implemented portal access changes, having sat on an access committee, having been the clinician asked to release a result you would rather have called the patient about first — abstract the experience and bring it into your post.

## Thread 3 — Ransomware preparedness — what your institution actually does and what it should do

Without identifying the institution, describe what your current institution (or your most recent one) actually does to prepare for a multi-week ransomware-induced downtime. Concretely: are downtime drills run? When was the last one? Are paper backup procedures current and printed on the units? Is there a clinical content packet stocked in physical form on each unit? Has the institution rehearsed the decision to take the EHR offline as a containment measure? Does anyone outside IT know what the activation criteria are?

Then say what the institution *should* do that it does not. Be honest. The point of the thread is not to brag about good practice or to embarrass institutions with bad practice — it is to surface the gap between the SAFER High Priority Practices Guide (Module 5) and the actual operational state of most U.S. hospitals, and to push each member of the cohort to take the gap personally. The boards test the topic; the field has been bad at it; the institution that fixes the gap before the next ransomware crew shows up is the institution whose patients are safer.

The standard wrong move on this thread is to recite the textbook answer about defense-in-depth without actually describing what your institution does. The grading rewards the candor.

## Posting expectations

- One top-level post on at least one thread.
- One reply on a different thread.
- Quote what you are responding to. "I disagree" is a 1 on the rubric.
- PHI hygiene is graded. One slip is a 1 regardless of the rest of the post.
- Substance matters more than length. A two-sentence reframe scores higher than a five-paragraph restatement.
- Do not name your institution or your colleagues. Abstract before you post.
