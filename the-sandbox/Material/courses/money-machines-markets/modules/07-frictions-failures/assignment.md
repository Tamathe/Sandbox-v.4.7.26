# Assignment — A Steel-Manned Critique of Bitcoin, of Frontier AI, and of Yourself

## The prompt

Write 900 words (±150) divided into three sections of roughly equal length:

1. **The strongest case against Bitcoin** that you, personally, can construct. Not the lazy version. Not the version a hostile journalist would write. The version that a brilliant, fair-minded skeptic — someone who has read the same lessons you have read in this course — would actually defend. You should make it as strong as you can. If at the end you still hold a pro-Bitcoin position, that is fine; the exercise is the steel-manning, not the conclusion.

2. **The strongest case against frontier AI** as it is currently being built and deployed. Same standard. Not "AI will steal jobs" or "the models hallucinate." Those are real but they are not the strong version. The strong version names a specific structural reason the current trajectory might be a mistake — the capability frontier mistaking benchmark performance for intelligence, the centralization of training in a handful of firms, the alignment problem being malformed, the data exhaustion curve, the regulatory capture loop from Lesson 3, something else you can defend.

3. **The strongest case against yourself for being interested in either.** This is the part that matters most. Why are *you*, specifically, drawn to this stuff? Be honest. Possible answers include but are not limited to: contrarian identity, fear of missing out, intellectual aesthetics, financial self-interest, ideological alignment with a particular political tribe, the social rewards of being early, the search for a coherent worldview in a confusing decade. Pick the answer that feels most uncomfortable to write down and write it down. Then ask: if that motivation is doing more work than the object-level arguments, would you still hold the same views? Be specific. Generic self-criticism does not count.

You may write the three sections in any order. The third one is the hardest. Most students will be tempted to write it last and shortest. Resist that. Give it at least as much room as the other two.

## What I'm looking for

This is graded against the **Written Thesis Rubric** (`rubrics/written-thesis.json`). The four criteria are:

1. **Clarity of claim** — Each section should have a defensible thesis that a reader can summarize in one sentence.
2. **Quality of reasoning** — The case in each section should be the strongest version *you* can construct, not a strawman, and the reasoning should be your own.
3. **Engagement with the strongest counter-argument** — In each section, name the counter-argument that would most threaten the case you just made, and say why a thoughtful person could still hold the case anyway. Yes, this means meta-steel-manning.
4. **Voice and honesty** — I want to hear *you*. Specifically, in section three, I want to hear the voice that talks to itself when nobody is watching. Generic essays will fail this criterion.

## What will sink it

- Three sections that all conclude "...but actually, on balance, I still think the technology is good." That is not a steel-manned critique. That is a defense in a costume. Either commit to making the critique strong, or notice that you cannot, and write *that* as your section.
- A third section that is generic ("I might be biased, like everyone is biased") rather than specific ("I am drawn to Bitcoin because I want to be the kind of person who saw something the mainstream missed, and that desire is doing about half the work my arguments are pretending to do").
- AI-generated prose. I will recognize it. Especially in the third section, where the voice has to be yours and the model's voice on this kind of self-reflection is unmistakable.
- A critique of Bitcoin or AI that does not engage with anything specific from the lessons. The vocabulary of this module — bezzle, contextual integrity, marginal emissions, Knightian uncertainty, regulatory capture, model collapse — should appear where it actually applies.
- Performative humility. "Of course I might be wrong" written 14 times is not honesty. It is a hedge.

## Format

- 900 words (±150)
- Plain markdown, three labeled sections
- A one-line note at the end disclosing any AI tool you used and how. ("Used Claude as a sparring partner on section two." That's enough.) For section three specifically, AI use is permitted only as a sparring partner, never as a drafter — if you let a model write the self-critique for you, the assignment will not have done its job for you.

## Why this assignment

Every prior module asked you to *defend* something — your view of money, your read on Bitcoin, your model of AI, your macro thesis. This module is the only one that asks you to *attack*, and to attack the things you have spent six modules building. The reason is simple: a view that you cannot turn against yourself is not a view, it is a flag. And the rest of the course (including the synthesis module) will be much better if you have spent 900 honest words finding out where your own flag is too tightly held.

If you finish this assignment and you have not changed your mind about anything, either you started in an extremely well-calibrated place or — much more likely — you flinched somewhere in the writing. Reread section three. The flinch is usually there.
