## Reading

The previous four lessons have made AI sound like a software story. This one is the corrective. **AI in 2026 is a physical-infrastructure story dressed in software clothes**, and the parts of it that constrain what is possible — what gets built, who gets to build it, where, and how fast — are not the algorithms. They are chips, electricity, water, and geopolitics. Anyone who tells you the next five years of AI will be determined by clever architectural breakthroughs is partially right. Anyone who tells you it will be determined by physical capacity is more right.

Let's walk the supply chain, because most people don't know how it actually works and this is the part the marketing always elides.

**Step one: chips.** A modern AI training cluster runs on specialized accelerators — almost always GPUs (graphics processing units) repurposed for matrix math, though Google has its own TPUs and a few startups make alternatives. The dominant supplier in 2026 is still **Nvidia**, whose H100 (2023), H200 (2024), B100/B200 (2024–2025), and Rubin-series chips (2026) define what "frontier compute" means and what it costs. Nvidia's revenue went from $27 billion in 2022 to over $130 billion in 2025, almost entirely on the strength of selling these chips to the AI labs and the cloud providers who serve them. Nvidia's market cap briefly made it the most valuable company in the world in 2024 and 2025. None of this is because Nvidia is a software company; it is because nobody else makes a comparable chip in volume, and the labs need them now, not in three years.

**Step two: who makes the chips Nvidia designs.** Nvidia does not manufacture its own chips. It designs them and sends the designs to a single fab company in a single country: **TSMC**, in Taiwan. TSMC makes the most advanced chips on earth, by a margin large enough that Samsung and Intel — the two other leading-edge fabs — are years behind on the cutting-edge process nodes (3nm in 2024, 2nm in 2025–26). TSMC's customers include Nvidia, Apple, AMD, Broadcom, and most of the rest of the high-end semiconductor industry. This concentration is not an accident of marketing. Building a leading-edge fab takes a decade and twenty to forty billion dollars per facility. The number of organizations on earth that can do it is small enough to count on one hand.

**Step three: the equipment that lets TSMC do it.** TSMC's fabs run on machines made by a Dutch company called **ASML**, which produces the only extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography systems on earth capable of etching the patterns required for sub-7nm chips. Each EUV machine costs around $200 million and weighs roughly 180 tons. There is exactly one supplier. When ASML can't ship, TSMC can't expand. When TSMC can't expand, Nvidia can't produce more chips. When Nvidia can't produce more chips, the AI labs can't train bigger models. The entire frontier AI capability of the world ultimately funnels through one Dutch company in Veldhoven that almost nobody outside the industry has heard of.

This three-step chain — Nvidia design, TSMC manufacture, ASML enable — is the *first* bottleneck, and it is the one that the public conversation has finally caught up to. The interesting bottlenecks are downstream.

**Step four: power.** A single frontier AI training cluster in 2026 draws on the order of **100 to 500 megawatts** of continuous electrical power. For reference, a typical American city of 100,000 people draws about 150 megawatts. The largest training clusters currently being built — Microsoft's, Meta's, xAI's, OpenAI's via various partners — are aiming for the gigawatt range, which is the scale of a small nuclear reactor. The bottleneck is not "is there enough power on the grid in aggregate." The bottleneck is "is there enough power available in one place, on a stable enough connection, with enough cooling capacity, to run this cluster for years without interruption." In most US locations the answer is no, and the time to build a new substation, transformer line, or grid connection is measured in years. Several of the planned 2026 frontier clusters are blocked not on chips but on grid interconnects. This is the single most important AI bottleneck nobody outside the industry talks about. The story of AI in 2027 will partly be a story of power-grid politics in West Texas, the Pacific Northwest, Iceland, the Gulf states, and a few other places where land, energy, and political will line up.

**Step five: water.** Modern data centers use enormous amounts of water for cooling, especially evaporative cooling in hot climates. A single hyperscale data center can consume on the order of 5 million gallons per day. This has triggered local political fights — in Arizona, in Spain, in Ireland, in Chile — wherever a data center sits next to a community that uses the same aquifer. Water is the second sleeper bottleneck. It binds before grid power in some regions and after it in others. Most of the public AI conversation ignores it entirely, which is exactly how you can tell the public conversation is downstream of marketing rather than upstream of operations.

**Step six: geopolitics.** Layered on top of all of this is a slow trade war whose central piece is the US export controls on advanced chips and chip-making equipment to China. Since October 2022 — and tightened repeatedly since — Nvidia cannot legally sell its top-end accelerators to Chinese customers. ASML cannot ship its top-end EUV machines to China. The US is openly trying to maintain a several-year lead in frontier AI capability over China, and the lever it is pulling is *physical access to the supply chain*. Not algorithms — algorithms travel through papers and weights and people. Hardware. The bet is that AI capability has become enough of a function of compute that bottlenecking compute is bottlenecking capability. The Chinese response — domestic chip programs, smuggled hardware, distillation of US-trained open models — has had partial success. Whether the bottleneck holds for another decade or whether China catches up by 2028 is the most consequential single question in the geopolitics of technology, and it depends almost entirely on physical infrastructure timelines, not on which side has smarter researchers.

Now zoom out, because the point of the lesson is about to land. Every framing of AI you encounter can be sorted by whether it acknowledges this physical layer. The "AI is just software, it'll be everywhere overnight" framing ignores the physical layer and is wrong. The "AI is uniquely concentrated in a few labs forever" framing partially ignores that the *physical* bottleneck is what makes the lab concentration sticky, not the algorithm secrets. The "China will catch up next year" framing underweights the physical lead time on fabs and grid. The "China will never catch up" framing underweights that physical things can be built, given enough money and time, by determined adversaries. Every claim about the future of AI is, downstream, a claim about chips, power, water, and a small number of choke points. If you find yourself reading an essay about the next five years of AI that doesn't mention any of those four words, the essay is fiction even if it is well-written.

## Concrete example

In 2024, Microsoft and OpenAI announced a planned $100 billion AI training cluster, codenamed Stargate, intended to come online in 2028. The headline number — $100 billion — is impressive. The interesting numbers are the ones nobody put in the headline. To run that cluster you need somewhere around 5 gigawatts of dedicated, stable electrical power, which is roughly the output of five large nuclear reactors. You need a site with the cooling capacity to dissipate the heat that 5 gigawatts of compute produces, which is roughly the cooling capacity of a small city. You need land. You need permits. You need enough fiber to backhaul training-scale data. And you need it all in one place, because a training cluster cannot be meaningfully spread across continents. As of early 2026, the Stargate project has been progressing on the chip side and visibly struggling on the power side, with multiple announcements about potential nuclear reactor partnerships, gas turbines on site, and unconventional financing of grid upgrades. The bottleneck on the most ambitious AI project in the world right now is not the AI. It is the wattage. Sit with that.

## Uncomfortable question

If the actual constraint on AI capability in 2026 is the physical infrastructure, then most of the public debate about AI — about jobs, about safety, about regulation, about who builds the smartest model — is happening in the wrong vocabulary. The questions that will determine outcomes are about grid permits, water rights, fab capacity, and export-control diplomacy. The questions that get the headlines are about model benchmarks and corporate drama. Why do you think the gap exists, and which conversation are you getting your beliefs from? It is possible to follow this field intelligently for years and never read a substantive piece about substations. That should bother you a little.
