## Reading

The phrase "hard money" gets thrown around constantly in Bitcoin discussions, usually with very little definition. Let's fix that, because the concept is older than Bitcoin and survives whether or not Bitcoin does.

**Hard money is money whose supply is hard to expand.** That's it. Not "money that holds its value" — that's a *consequence*, sometimes, but not the definition. The defining property is: when the price of the money goes up, can people *make more of it* easily? If yes, it is soft. If no, it is hard.

A useful way to think about this is the **stock-to-flow ratio**:

- **Stock**: how much of the thing exists in the world right now.
- **Flow**: how much new supply is added per year.
- **Ratio**: stock divided by flow. Higher means harder.

For gold, the existing above-ground stock is something like 200,000 tonnes. Annual mine production is roughly 3,000 tonnes. The stock-to-flow ratio is around 60. Translation: even if every gold mine on earth ran flat-out for a year, the supply of gold would only grow by about 1.7%. Gold is hard money. It has been hard money for thousands of years, which is exactly why human societies kept gravitating back to it across very different cultures with very different politics.

For the US dollar, "stock" depends on which aggregate you use, but M2 grew by a long-run average of around 6–7% per year in normal times and *much* faster during stimulus episodes. The stock-to-flow ratio is in the low teens at best, and the flow can be increased dramatically by policy decisions in a short period. The dollar is soft money. Not catastrophically soft — most of the time the policy keeps things in check — but soft.

For Bitcoin in 2026, the stock is about 19.7 million BTC and the annual issuance is currently about 165,000 BTC (after the 2024 halving), giving a stock-to-flow ratio in the 110s. After the next halving (around April 2028) it will roughly double. After the one after that, it doubles again. Bitcoin is currently harder than gold by this measure, and it asymptotes toward infinity as the supply schedule runs to its hard cap of 21 million coins.

This stock-to-flow framing was popularized in the Bitcoin world by an analyst called PlanB, who used it to make price predictions that turned out to be partially right and partially very wrong. That doesn't matter for our purposes. What matters is the underlying property: **a money you can't easily make more of has very different incentive properties from a money you can.**

Why does it matter?

**First**, hard money rewards saving and punishes nothing. If the supply can't be expanded, the value of each unit tends to rise (or at least hold) over time as the economy around it grows. Holding the money is a perfectly reasonable economic strategy. You don't have to "put your money to work" to avoid losing value. This sounds nice on paper. The flip side, which Keynesians correctly point out, is that hard money discourages risk-taking and investment, and an economy where everyone hoards is a stagnant economy. There is a real tension here, and which side you weight depends on what you think the bigger problem is in any given era.

**Second**, hard money strips a tool out of the hands of governments. The tool is **seigniorage** — the profit a government makes by issuing money. When a government can print, it can fund itself by debasing the existing money supply, which is essentially a tax that nobody voted for and that hits savers disproportionately. Hard money makes this tax much harder to collect. Governments have to fund themselves by taxing visibly or borrowing, both of which generate political pushback. Hard money advocates see this as a feature: it forces fiscal honesty. Critics see it as a bug: a government that *can't* run deficits in a crisis can't respond to crises. Both are right.

**Third**, hard money tends to win when soft money is failing. This is the Thiers's Law point from Lesson 2. When local currencies are clearly being debased, people migrate. Historically they migrated to gold. In recent decades they have migrated to dollars. In some countries today they are migrating to stablecoins or Bitcoin. The migration is voluntary; the market sorts itself.

A crucial caveat that almost nobody acknowledges in either direction: **hardness is not the same as goodness, and softness is not the same as badness**. A hard money in the wrong context can crush an economy that needs flexibility. A soft money in a competent hands has powered some of the most prosperous decades in human history (the post-war US dollar before 1971 was on a quasi-gold standard but not strict; the period after 1971 has seen enormous real prosperity alongside enormous monetary expansion). Hard money is a *property*, not a *verdict*. The verdict depends on what you want money to do for the society that uses it.

The Bitcoin maximalist case rests on three claims, in order of how disputed they are:

1. **Hardness is a desirable property in money.** (Most reasonable people, including most economists, will grant some version of this.)
2. **The current monetary system is too soft, and the consequences are accumulating.** (Disputed but defensible — you can point to debt-to-GDP charts and asset price inflation as evidence.)
3. **Bitcoin specifically, rather than gold or some other hard asset, is the right place to migrate.** (This is the most contested claim, because it hinges on technological assumptions about Bitcoin's network surviving for decades, on no superior alternative emerging, and on enough of the world coordinating on it.)

If you find yourself arguing about Bitcoin with someone, figure out which of those three claims you actually disagree on. Most arguments are people disagreeing on (3) while pretending they disagree on (1).

There is also a fourth concept lurking here that we will use heavily later: **Schelling point**. A Schelling point is a solution that everyone gravitates to even without communicating, because everyone expects everyone else to gravitate to it. In a world where you can't trust the local money, the question "what do I migrate to?" has a Schelling-point quality. Historically, gold was the Schelling answer because everyone knew everyone else recognized it. Bitcoin's bet is that it can become the new Schelling point for digital monetary migration. Whether it does is an empirical question, not a logical one. It could win. It could plateau. It could lose. The market decides.

## Concrete example

In 2025, the Argentine peso lost approximately 60% of its dollar-equivalent value over the year. (By the time you read this, that number may be different — Argentina has been doing this on and off for decades.) During that year, Argentine demand for stablecoins (especially USDT and USDC) grew explosively. So did Argentine demand for Bitcoin, though to a lesser extent, because Bitcoin's volatility made it a less attractive *store of value* on a 12-month horizon than dollar-pegged stablecoins.

Notice what this case shows and what it doesn't. It shows that when local soft money fails, people seek harder alternatives. It does not show that they pick the absolute hardest available alternative. They pick the alternative that is hard *enough* for their time horizon while staying usable. For most Argentines in 2025, that meant digital dollars, not Bitcoin. The Bitcoin case in Argentina is stronger over a 5-year horizon and weaker over a 12-month horizon, which is exactly what you'd predict if you were thinking about this carefully.

This is also why Bitcoin maximalists who dismiss stablecoins as "fiat in a costume" are missing an important fact: stablecoins are *winning* the medium-of-exchange and short-term store-of-value competition in failing-currency economies. Bitcoin is winning the long-term store-of-value bet in some of those same economies. Both can be true at the same time. The world is not zero-sum.

## Uncomfortable question

If you genuinely believed that the dollar was going to lose 50% of its purchasing power over the next 20 years — not collapse, just slowly bleed — what would you change about your finances *this month*? Not in some hypothetical "if I had a million dollars" scenario, but with the actual money you have now? Most people, when they answer this honestly, find that they would do almost nothing different, which means one of two things is true: either they don't actually believe the premise, or they are making a deliberate choice to ignore the implications. Both are interesting. Neither is wrong. But it's worth knowing which one is true about you, because the rest of the course is going to keep poking at the same gap.
