## Reading

The textbook definition of money has three parts. Memorize them. The rest of the course returns to them constantly.

**Medium of exchange.** Money is the thing you hand over to get something else. It eliminates the coincidence-of-wants problem from the previous lesson. The test is friction: how easy is it to use this thing to settle a transaction *right now*, with someone you don't know, who doesn't owe you anything?

**Store of value.** Money is the thing you can hold today and still have tomorrow, next year, or in twenty years. The test is durability of purchasing power: if you put a dollar (or a Bitcoin, or a gold coin) in a drawer, what fraction of its current buying power is left when you open the drawer?

**Unit of account.** Money is the thing in which prices are quoted. The test is whether people instinctively reach for it when comparing the value of two things. In the United States, the unit of account is the dollar. Even Bitcoin holders, when asked "what is your house worth," answer in dollars. That is what unit-of-account dominance looks like.

The crucial thing — the thing the textbooks usually breeze past — is that **these three functions do not have to live in the same object**, and historically they often haven't.

In the late Roman Empire, you could pay your taxes in one coinage, your soldiers in another, and price your wheat in a third. In Argentina today, it is normal to *price* big-ticket items (cars, houses) in dollars, *transact* in pesos at the moment of sale because of legal requirements, and *save* in dollars or stablecoins or US treasuries. The medium of exchange is the peso. The store of value is the dollar. The unit of account is the dollar. Three functions, three different objects, all called "money" loosely.

This split tends to happen when the local currency fails one of the three functions badly. Almost always, the function that fails first is **store of value**, because inflation eats it. People keep transacting in the local money (because they have to) but they price and save in something more stable. If the failure gets worse, the unit of account migrates, then finally the medium of exchange. By the time even the medium of exchange has fled, the local currency has effectively died, even if it's still legally tender.

Two old laws describe this:

**Gresham's Law**, often summarized as "bad money drives out good." If two currencies circulate at a forced exchange rate, people spend the bad one and hoard the good one. Why? Because they would rather pay debts in something that's losing value and save in something that's holding it. The good money disappears from circulation. You see this whenever a government tries to peg an overvalued currency.

**Thiers's Law**, the inverse, sometimes called the "anti-Gresham": when the exchange rate isn't forced — when people can *choose* what to use — they migrate toward the better money. Argentina is a Thiers's Law country today. People are not forced to use dollars; they prefer to. The dollar is winning the store-of-value competition by free choice.

Why does any of this matter for the rest of the course?

Because when people argue about Bitcoin, they usually argue past each other by collapsing the three functions into one. A Bitcoin maximalist says "Bitcoin is money." A skeptic says "no it isn't, you can't buy coffee with it." They are both right and both wrong, because they are evaluating Bitcoin against different functions. As a *medium of exchange* in 2026, Bitcoin is mediocre — slow, expensive on the base layer, weird tax treatment, narrow merchant acceptance. As a *store of value* over 10+ year windows, Bitcoin has, so far, outperformed nearly everything. As a *unit of account*, it is essentially nowhere. People who hold Bitcoin still price their lives in dollars.

The honest framing is: Bitcoin is currently strong on one function, weak on another, and absent on the third. Whether you think that matters depends on which function you think a society needs hardest right now. Reasonable people disagree.

The same framing applies to stablecoins, which are a fascinating case: they are *excellent* mediums of exchange in certain corridors (cross-border, dollar-denominated, within crypto), *decent* short-term stores of value (they target $1, mostly hold), and a *non-existent* unit of account (no one prices in USDC; they price in the dollar that USDC tracks). Stablecoins are essentially a parasite on the dollar's unit-of-account function — and it works.

And it applies to the dollar itself. The dollar is currently dominant on all three functions globally, but the *strength* of its store-of-value function has been weakening for decades, slowly, in ways that are visible if you measure them in basket-of-goods or hours-of-labor terms rather than in nominal dollars. Whether this weakening is fatal, terminal but slow, or normal depends on who you ask. It is not a settled question.

## Concrete example

A friend of mine in Buenos Aires gets paid in pesos. Within minutes of being paid, she converts her salary to USDT (a dollar-pegged stablecoin) on her phone. She uses a debit card linked to a peso account for groceries, but she keeps almost no peso balance. When she shops for an apartment, the listings are in dollars. The seller will accept dollars, USDT, or the peso equivalent at the unofficial rate, but never the official rate.

In her life, three different objects perform the three functions of money. She does not think of this as exotic. She thinks of it as basic financial hygiene in a country where the local currency loses 80% of its value some years. She is also, without realizing it, a perfect case study in what happens when one currency fails and a population sorts itself across replacements function by function.

## Uncomfortable question

What is the worst-performing function of the dollar in your life right now? Be specific. Don't say "inflation is bad." Say *what dollar-priced thing has gotten more expensive in a way that surprised you in the last five years, and by how much, and what would have to change for you to start storing your savings in something other than dollars*.

Most people, if they answer this honestly, find that the dollar is failing them quietly on store-of-value while still doing fine on the other two. That's the situation we are actually in. Hold the answer. We come back to it in Module 6.
