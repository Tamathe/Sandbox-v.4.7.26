## Reading

Five lessons of engineering. Now the part that the engineering lessons cannot avoid: Bitcoin is a political artifact. This is not a fringe interpretation. It is what the system *is*, regardless of whether you find that thrilling or annoying. The genesis block, mined in January 2009, contains a hidden message in its coinbase data: *"The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks."* This is not a technical field. It is not necessary for the system to function. It is a political statement, embedded by Satoshi at block zero, dating Bitcoin's creation against a specific event in the history of the response to the 2008 financial crisis. Whatever else Bitcoin became, it began as a protest.

The protest was specific and narrow. It was not "all government is bad." It was not "abolish central banks." It was something more like: *the existing system of banks-backed-by-states-backed-by-money-printers has produced a recurring failure mode where the banks privatize the gains and socialize the losses, and the holders of the currency pay the cost. Here is an alternative that does not depend on any of that.* You can disagree with the diagnosis. You can disagree with the proposed remedy. But you cannot understand the system if you pretend the protest isn't there.

The technical properties of Bitcoin all have political consequences, and they were chosen with those consequences in mind:

**Fixed supply** is not just a monetary property; it is a refusal to grant any authority the power to debase the currency. Whether you think that authority is being well- or badly-used in the fiat system, the choice to remove it is a political choice.

**Permissionless participation** — anyone can run a node, anyone can mine, anyone can transact, anyone can receive — is not just an accessibility property; it is a refusal to allow gatekeeping. Real existing financial systems gate-keep enormously, often for reasons that are individually defensible (KYC, sanctions enforcement, fraud prevention) and collectively very powerful (it is hard to be financially active if you cannot get a bank account, and some categories of people cannot get bank accounts). Bitcoin's design says "no, this layer doesn't gate-keep." That is a political position even if the people implementing it think they're being apolitical.

**Censorship resistance** — the property that no one can prevent a valid transaction from being included on the chain forever — is not just a technical property; it is a refusal of state authority to intermediate every monetary act. Sanctions regimes, asset freezes, and account closures all become harder when the underlying medium will not enforce them. Whether this is good or bad depends on whose sanctions, whose freezes, whose closures. The same property that lets a Russian dissident move money out of Russia also lets a sanctions evader move money into Russia. The property does not distinguish. That non-distinction is itself political: it says "we trust the rules of the protocol more than we trust any authority's case-by-case judgments."

The block-size war of 2015–2017 is the moment Bitcoin discovered, in public, that it had no formal governance and that its real governance was social. Briefly: there was a long-running debate about whether to increase Bitcoin's block size limit (to allow more transactions per block, which would allow more on-chain throughput, at the cost of making nodes more expensive to run). One faction (the "big blockers," supported by some major mining pools and prominent early developers) wanted to increase it. The other faction (the "small blockers," supported by most of the core developer community and many node operators) wanted to keep it small and scale via second-layer solutions like Lightning. There was no formal vote. There was no constitution. There was an extended, often ugly, public fight that played out on forums, mailing lists, conferences, and Twitter, eventually resulting in a fork: the big-blocker side split off and created Bitcoin Cash, while the original chain — now backed by the small blockers and most node operators — kept the name Bitcoin and followed the small-block path.

What the block-size war established, in retrospect, is the **principle of social consensus**: changes to Bitcoin require coordination among independently-acting node operators, miners, developers, exchanges, and users, and any change that fails to achieve broad coordination simply doesn't happen — even if a majority of one faction wants it. This is not a feature of any single rule in the code. It is an emergent property of the architecture. The same mechanism that prevents a central authority from changing the rules also makes it very hard to change the rules at all. This is sometimes called **ossification** and Bitcoin is, by design or by accident, on a slow path toward becoming functionally unchangeable. The maximalists call this a feature ("calcifying the protocol means nobody can corrupt it"). Critics call it a bug ("a protocol that cannot evolve cannot fix its own problems, including the security budget problem"). Both are right about what they see; they disagree about which thing matters more.

The contested claim that comes out of all this is that Bitcoin represents a **separation of money and state**, the way the Enlightenment produced a separation of church and state. This is a big claim and worth taking seriously rather than dismissing or accepting. Here is the strongest version of it: monetary issuance and monetary policy are quasi-religious functions in modern states — they involve a small priesthood, opaque rituals, and a faith-based relationship between the citizenry and the institutions. Removing the state from money does not remove money; it just removes the authority that previously claimed the right to manage it. In the same way, removing the state from religion did not abolish religion — it created a space where religious affiliation became voluntary, plural, and competitive. By analogy, removing the state from money would create a space where monetary affiliation becomes voluntary, plural, and competitive. People could choose whichever money best fit their needs, without coercion. Bitcoin would not be "the money"; it would be one money in a competitive landscape, and its persistence would depend on continuing to be the best at what it does.

That is the strongest version of the claim. Now the strongest critique: separation of church and state worked because most people, most of the time, did not need the state to enforce their religious choices for them. Money is different. Money is the substrate of taxation, of contracts, of debt, of legal enforcement, of collective action. A state that cannot denominate its taxes in a money it controls is a state that cannot fund itself except by coercion in some other denomination, and historically, states do not give up coercive tools voluntarily. The "separation" framing assumes the state will accept the demotion the way it eventually accepted the demotion of religious authority. There is no historical precedent for a state voluntarily giving up its monetary tools, and the cases where states have *lost* monetary control (Weimar, Argentina at various points, Zimbabwe) are not cases of voluntary separation; they are cases of monetary collapse that was extremely painful to live through. The critique is: even if Bitcoin is technically capable of serving as separation-money, the political path from here to there runs through state opposition, and the maximalists tend to wave that path away.

Notice that you can take the technical lessons of Module 2 and the political claims here, and assemble them into very different overall positions. You can think Bitcoin is engineering-genius and politically-doomed. You can think it is engineering-fragile and politically-essential. You can think it is both right-on-the-merits and likely to fail. None of these positions are foolish. The position that is foolish is the one that pretends the political dimension isn't there at all, in either direction. *Bitcoin is just a technology, leave politics out of it* is wrong because the technology was built around a political position. *Bitcoin is just a movement, the technical details don't matter* is wrong because the movement only works to the extent the technology actually does what it claims. You have to hold both at once.

## Concrete example

In 2022, the Canadian government, responding to the trucker convoy protests in Ottawa, invoked emergency powers to freeze the bank accounts and credit card processing of people associated with the protest — including donors who had given small amounts. This was done without judicial review, retroactively, and in some cases targeted people who had not been charged with any crime. Whatever you think of the protest itself, the freeze was a vivid demonstration of what financial intermediation makes possible: when money flows through accounts that an authority can reach, that authority can disable those accounts.

During the same period, there was an attempt to crowdfund the convoy via Bitcoin. The attempt ran into its own difficulties — most of the on-ramps and off-ramps (exchanges) were themselves intermediated and could comply with freeze orders, and the funds that did reach the protest were partially seized at the exchange layer. But the *base layer* of Bitcoin was untouchable by the order. Donations sent peer-to-peer reached their destinations. The base layer behaved exactly as advertised.

This is a case where the technical property and the political property are inseparable. A Bitcoin that *could* be censored at the base layer would not have routed those donations. A Bitcoin that *can't* be censored is one that will route donations to causes you support and to causes you oppose, equally and without distinction. This is the point. The property is general. You don't get the protected dissident without also getting the protected sanctions-evader. If you find that trade-off comfortable, you are probably underestimating one side. If you find it unacceptable, you are probably underestimating the other. The interesting question is which side of the trade-off you weight more, and why, and whether you would still answer the same way if the political valence of the case were flipped.

## Uncomfortable question

Imagine your political opponents successfully use Bitcoin's censorship resistance to do something you find deeply objectionable — funding a cause you consider dangerous, evading sanctions you support, moving money in a way that bypasses laws you think are good. Do you, in that moment, still defend the protocol's neutrality? The honest answer for most people is "I'd want an exception for this case," and the honest follow-up is that *every* attempt to add exceptions to a censorship-resistant system destroys exactly the property that made it valuable to its defenders in the first place. The protocol does not give you the option of being selectively permissive. You either commit to its neutrality knowing you will sometimes hate the consequences, or you ask for changes that bring back the gatekeeping you originally objected to. This is the same trade-off free-speech absolutists wrestle with, and most people who claim to be absolutists are not, when tested. Where exactly is your line? Try to write it down, and notice how arbitrary the line feels once you do.
