## Reading

There are two related problems in the AI era that look like they call for crypto-shaped solutions, and one of them genuinely does. We're going to sort them.

**Problem one: content provenance.** As generative models get better, we lose the ability to tell at a glance whether a photo, video, or document was produced by a human or by a model. The naive response is "we will train detectors." That has not worked and almost certainly cannot work in the long run, because every detector creates a gradient that the next generation of models is implicitly trained against. The arms race favors generation, not detection. If you want to know who made a piece of content, you cannot reliably figure it out from the content alone. You have to attach the answer at the moment of creation.

This is what **content provenance** systems do. The most developed standard is **C2PA** (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity), backed by Adobe, Microsoft, the BBC, and others. The idea is that capture devices and editing tools cryptographically sign the content and the chain of edits at each step. A photo from a C2PA-enabled camera carries a signature from the camera; an edit in a C2PA-aware editor adds a signature describing the edit; the result is a signed history that follows the file. A viewer can verify the chain and see "this image was captured by a Sony camera at this GPS location at this time, then cropped and color-corrected in Photoshop, and has not been otherwise altered." Or: "this image was generated by Midjourney v8 at this date, via this prompt, and has not been altered since."

A few things to notice. First, **C2PA is mostly cryptography, not blockchain.** The signatures use standard X.509-style PKI. The chain (in the sense of "blockchain") is only sometimes involved, usually as an anchoring layer for revocation lists or as a public registry of signing keys. A purist would say content provenance is not really an "AI + crypto" topic — it's an AI + cryptographic-signing topic, which is older and less hyped. Fair. But the public-registry piece, the part where you want anyone to be able to verify a signing key without trusting a central authority, *is* something a chain does well, and several of the production C2PA deployments use one for that purpose.

Second, **provenance does not prove truth.** A photo can be honestly captured by a camera and still misleading — staged scenes, deceptive captions, selective framing. Provenance proves *origin and edit history*. It does not promote the contents to "true." Anyone selling provenance as a fix for misinformation is misrepresenting what it does. What it does is more modest and more useful: it gives the people who care about authenticity a way to check origin, and it gives honest creators a way to assert "this is mine and unaltered" in a way that can be verified mechanically.

Third, **watermarking is a much weaker primitive and you should not confuse the two.** A watermark is a perturbation of the content (visible or invisible) that is supposed to identify the producing model. Visible watermarks are trivially removed. Invisible watermarks are removed by any decent re-encoding, by minor edits, by adversarial filters specifically designed to scrub them. The major model providers have invested in watermarking schemes and the major model providers know that their watermarks survive a casual user but not a determined one. Watermarking is a polite agreement among compliant parties. Provenance is a verifiable cryptographic claim. In an adversarial setting, only the second one is load-bearing.

**Problem two: proof of human.** As AI agents proliferate, the fundamental "are you a real person" test that the internet has relied on (CAPTCHA, account history, IP reputation) is collapsing. Bots write better essays than humans; agents pass CAPTCHAs more reliably than tired humans do. For some applications this doesn't matter. For others — voting, social media one-person-one-account constraints, airdrops, government services, anti-sybil measures generally — it matters enormously. The whole concept of "one human, one account" requires a way to verify the *one human* part.

There are a small number of approaches and they all have ugly trade-offs.

**Government ID-based**: tie accounts to verified national IDs. Solves the technical problem; transfers the trust to whichever government issues the ID. Doesn't work for stateless people, doesn't work in low-trust regimes, and creates a single point of failure for surveillance. Works well in some contexts (banking) and badly in others (free speech).

**Web-of-trust**: humans vouch for other humans, and your status as a verified human is derived from the web of vouches. Decentralized, no central authority. Slow to bootstrap, vulnerable to coordinated inauthentic vouching, and hard to make rigorous.

**Biometric uniqueness**: verify the person's biometrics (iris, face) are unique in a database, without storing the biometrics themselves. This is the **World ID** approach (the project formerly known as Worldcoin). The math, using zero-knowledge proofs over a uniqueness set, is real and interesting. The hardware (the "Orb") requires physical presence at a verification device. The tradeoffs are: it works (people who have been Orbed can prove personhood without revealing identity), it scales (millions of verifications globally), and it raises serious questions (about the company's data practices, about the geopolitical concentration of verification infrastructure, about consent and incentives among populations being paid to verify).

I will say my honest view of World ID specifically: the cryptography is solid, the use case (sybil resistance for large-scale online systems) is real and growing, and the institutional and ethical questions are genuine but are not disqualifying. It is the only proof-of-human system that has actually shipped at scale, and the alternatives are either "trust the government" or "trust nobody and live with the bots." If you reject World ID, you should be ready to say which of those two alternatives you prefer, and why. Most critics of World ID are not.

What ties provenance and proof of human together is that they are both responses to the same underlying shift: **as software gets better at producing content and acting on behalf of (or instead of) humans, the assumption that "you can tell a person from a bot, and you can tell an original from a fake" stops being reliable, and we need new infrastructure to recover what we used to get for free.** Some of that infrastructure is cryptographic. Some of it lives on chains. Some of it is just clever PKI. The category as a whole is important enough that you should be able to distinguish the parts.

## Concrete example

In 2025, a major news organization launched a "verified original" badge on its photo journalism. Every photo published with the badge carries C2PA metadata anchored to the news organization's signing key, which is in turn anchored on a public chain so that revocation is verifiable without trusting the news organization itself. A viewer with a compatible browser extension can click any photo and see the full chain: which photographer, which camera, which edits, which date.

In the first six months, two interesting things happened. First, a lot of viewers ignored the badge, because most viewers do not actually care about provenance until they have been burned. Second, a small number of viewers in adversarial contexts (legal, political, archival) used the badge intensively, because for them the verifiable chain was the difference between admissible evidence and rumor. The badge was not transformative for the median reader. It was indispensable for the marginal reader who needed it. That ratio is what most provenance products will look like for the next several years, and it is also why they will be undersold to investors and underbuilt relative to their actual social value.

## Uncomfortable question

If you accept that provenance and proof-of-human are real and useful primitives, you have to accept that you are arguing for *more* identity verification and *more* signing infrastructure, in a world that is already uncomfortable with how much of both we have. Where exactly do you draw the line — what *should* be signable and verifiable, and what should remain unverifiable on purpose? "All of it" is an answer with consequences. "None of it" is an answer with different consequences. Sketch yours.
