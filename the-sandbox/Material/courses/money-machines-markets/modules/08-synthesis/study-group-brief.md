# Module 8 — Study Group: The Thesis Roundtable

## Format

Groups of three. (Four if your cohort doesn't divide cleanly. Not five — five is too many to give every thesis its time.) Each person brings the **draft thesis statement** they wrote for the module assignment. The session is structured so that every person gets the same amount of attention and the same kind of pressure. Nobody is allowed to dominate. Nobody is allowed to hide.

## What you bring

- One printed copy of your draft thesis statement (a single sentence or short paragraph) and your three-bullet outline from the assignment.
- Bring it on **paper**. No screens during the session except one shared timer. The reason is that it is much harder to hide a weak sentence on paper than on a screen, and it is much easier to mark up someone else's draft when you have a pen in your hand.

## Structure (60 minutes total)

1. **Round-robin reading (10 min)** — Each person reads their thesis statement out loud, in turn, with no commentary from the other two. No questions, no reactions, no nodding. Just read. This is awkward and that is the point: it strips away the social cushioning and leaves the sentence standing on its own.
2. **First focus (15 min)** — Pick one person (the one whose name comes first alphabetically by first name). The other two have 15 minutes to do exactly three things, in this order:
   - **Summarize back** what they think the person's thesis is, in one sentence each. The person being workshopped does not respond yet — they just listen. If both summaries are different from each other, the thesis is not yet clear enough to be summarized. That is information.
   - **Name the load-bearing claim** — the single sentence in the thesis that, if it turned out to be wrong, would bring the rest down with it. There is always one. Help find it.
   - **Name the strongest counter-argument** — not the obvious one, the strongest one. The one a smart person who disagreed would actually use. The person being workshopped takes notes but does not defend yet.
3. **Second focus (15 min)** — Same protocol, second person.
4. **Third focus (15 min)** — Same protocol, third person.
5. **Closing pass (5 min)** — Each person, in turn, says one sentence about what they will change in their thesis based on the session. *One sentence.* No more. The discipline is the point — you should walk out with a single concrete change to make, not a vague feeling that "the session was helpful."

## Rules

- **No defending in the focus rounds.** When you are the person being workshopped, you take notes silently. You will be tempted to explain, qualify, and justify. Don't. Defending in the moment is the failure mode that prevents you from hearing the feedback. You will have plenty of time to revise after the session.
- **Summarize before you critique.** The first thing the other two do is *try to repeat the thesis back*. If they cannot repeat it accurately, the thesis is not clear, and that is more useful information than any critique.
- **Name the strongest counter-argument, not the easiest one.** Steel-manning is the rule. Anyone who points out an obvious objection ("but Bitcoin is volatile") instead of the deepest one ("but the network may not survive a 30-year time horizon, and your thesis depends on it surviving") has not done their job.
- **No phones. No screens.** One timer. Paper and pens only. The friction is doing work.
- **No "this is great" comments.** Encouragement without specifics is noise. If you want to praise something, name the *specific* sentence that is doing work and say *why* it is doing work. "Your second sentence is your strongest because it commits to a falsifiable observation" is useful. "I really liked it" is not.

## Why this format

Most people, after seven modules of this course, have a thesis in their head that *feels* sharp until they have to say it out loud to two other humans. The roundtable forces the move from felt-sharpness to actually-sharp. The summarize-back protocol is doing the heaviest lifting: it is the cheapest possible test of whether your sentence transmits, and almost every weak thesis fails it on the first try.

You will be tempted to skip the no-defending rule and to start arguing back during your focus round. Don't. The single most valuable thing you can do for yourself in this session is to take the criticism in silence and process it later. The two people across from you are the closest thing you will have to a calibrated peer review before the actual final project. Use them.

The closing-pass discipline (one sentence on what you will change) is the part most groups skip and most regret skipping. The point is to leave the room with a concrete commitment, not a feeling. Write the sentence down. Tape it to your monitor. Use it to drive your next revision pass. You will thank yourself in a week.
