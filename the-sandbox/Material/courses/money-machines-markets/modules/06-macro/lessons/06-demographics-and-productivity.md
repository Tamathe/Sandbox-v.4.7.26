## Reading

Almost every major developed economy is, right now, in a demographic situation that is unprecedented in peacetime history. The fertility rate required to maintain a stable population (without immigration) is about 2.1 children per woman. As of 2026, the United States is around 1.6, Western Europe averages around 1.4, China is around 1.0, South Korea is around 0.7, and Japan is around 1.2 and has been below replacement for so long that its population is actively shrinking by hundreds of thousands per year. These are not forecasts. They are the realized fertility rates from the last few years, and they are baked into the population pyramid for decades to come because the women who would have had those missing children are not going to materialize.

This is the slow-motion fact at the bottom of almost every macro story you will encounter for the rest of your life. It changes which technologies matter, which fiscal regimes are sustainable, which countries are going to grow, and what "labor market" even means in an environment where the labor force is actively contracting.

Let me give you the arithmetic before the implications. The **dependency ratio** is the ratio of people not in the working-age range (under 15 or over 65) to people in it. In 1950, the US dependency ratio was about 0.55 — every working-age person supported about half a dependent. In 2026, it's about 0.65. By 2050, on current trajectories, it crosses 0.80 in the US, 1.0 in Italy and Japan, and approaches the unthinkable in Korea. China's number is rising even faster than Japan's, from a much higher peak working-age share. The "demographic dividend" that powered the East Asian growth miracle is not just over; it has reversed. The same demographic structure that gave you a country full of young workers in 1985 gives you a country full of retirees in 2035.

What does this do to growth? In the long run, GDP per capita growth is approximately the sum of two things: **labor productivity growth** (how much each worker can produce) and the **growth in the share of the population that works**. When the second number goes negative — which it now is in most of the OECD and all of East Asia — you need *all* of your growth to come from productivity growth. If productivity growth is slow (as it has been in most developed economies since 2005, the so-called "productivity slowdown" or **secular stagnation** debate), then aggregate GDP starts to flatten. And aggregate GDP, not GDP per capita, is what services the debt from Lesson 1 and pays the entitlements of the now-much-larger retired population. The arithmetic of pension promises and medical entitlements made in the 1960s simply does not work in a population with this dependency ratio at this productivity growth rate. Something has to give. Either the promises shrink, or the working population pays more, or you find some way to dramatically increase productivity per worker. The first two options are politically unacceptable everywhere they have been tried. The third option is where AI re-enters the story.

Here is where this lesson ties everything in this module together. We have:

- A debt overhang that requires growth to service (Lesson 1).
- An energy base layer that constrains growth (Lesson 2).
- A labor force in which cognitive jobs are getting cheaper but the entry pipeline is being broken (Lesson 3).
- Capital and compute that concentrate (Lesson 4).
- A monetary system in three-way fragmentation between CBDCs, stablecoins, and Bitcoin (Lesson 5).
- A working-age population that is shrinking absolutely, while the retired population grows (this lesson).

Two of these — labor (Lesson 3) and demographics (this lesson) — point in opposite directions on the *AI-and-jobs* question, and the apparent contradiction is actually the most important framing in the whole module. Lesson 3 said: AI is going to displace large categories of cognitive workers, and the political problem is what to do with the people whose jobs collapse. This lesson says: there is a shortage of working-age people that is going to get rapidly worse, and economies *desperately need* productivity-multiplying technology to keep aggregate output from falling. Both are true. The reconciliation is that **AI is simultaneously a substitute for missing workers and a competitor to surviving ones**, and which of these two things AI is in any given context depends on whether the local economy has enough workers or not enough.

In Japan in 2026, automation is overwhelmingly a *substitute*. There are not enough humans to do the work. Robotic elder-care, automated convenience stores, lights-out factories — none of these are taking jobs from humans who want them, because the humans are not there. Japan races to automate because it has to. The political backlash is essentially zero. The political concern is "are we automating fast enough to keep the lights on?"

In the US in 2026, the picture is mixed. There is a labor shortage in skilled trades, healthcare, and manufacturing, where automation is a substitute. There is a labor surplus (or about-to-be-surplus) in entry-level white-collar work, where automation is a competitor (Lesson 3). The political dynamics are confused precisely because both effects operate in the same country at the same time, and the same person can be a beneficiary of one and a victim of the other.

In a country with very young demographics — most of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of South Asia — automation is a competitor, because labor is plentiful and the economic problem is finding work for people, not finding people for work. The AI-and-development question is harder than the "leapfrog" narratives suggest, because the scaling-laws economics from Lesson 4 mean frontier models will not be built locally and the labor-displacement effects from Lesson 3 will land on workers without any buffer.

The aging-society-and-productivity story is also where the **fiscal dominance** question from Lesson 1 stops being abstract. The entitlement promises that political systems made to their populations in the post-war period assumed roughly stable population pyramids. Those promises are now mostly unfunded against current trajectories. Political systems facing this have three options: tax the working population more (already deeply unpopular), break the promises to the elderly (politically suicidal in democracies because the elderly vote and the unborn don't), or inflate the value of the promises away (the path of least resistance, leading directly back to the fiscal-dominance question). The fourth option, the only honest one, is *raise productivity per worker enough to cover the gap*, which puts you in a position of *needing* AI to work as a productivity multiplier even if you are also worried about its labor-displacement effects.

This is the conceptual trap most political conversations about AI are stuck in. People want to be against AI because it threatens jobs *and* be in favor of solving the entitlement crisis without raising taxes or cutting benefits. Both of those positions cannot be coherent at the same time. If you don't get the productivity boost from AI, you can't fund the existing entitlement system. If you do get the productivity boost from AI, you have to figure out how to redistribute the gains from a system in which the gains accrue first to capital. There is no clean exit from this dilemma. There are only honest engagements with the trade-offs and dishonest ones. Most public discourse is dishonest, on all sides, because the honest version is uncomfortable for everyone.

Tie this back to Module 1 one last time. Money is, at the level we have been discussing it, a mechanism for coordinating claims on production. When the production base shrinks and the claims grow, the money becomes the place where the conflict shows up — through inflation, through asset price movements, through monetary fragmentation, through the migration of savings into harder forms. Everything in this course has been preparing you to read those signals correctly. Demographics is the slow-moving constraint that conditions all of them. It is the only macro variable we can forecast 30 years out with high confidence, and almost nobody plans around it, which is exactly why it is going to dominate the next three decades of macro that get written.

## Concrete example

South Korea in 2025: total fertility rate of about 0.72, the lowest of any country in human history during peacetime. The elementary school in many rural districts has more teachers than students. Universities are closing. Conscription is being shortened because there are not enough conscription-age men. The Korean government is simultaneously running the most aggressive industrial policy in the OECD around AI, robotics, and semiconductor manufacturing — *because it has no other option*. There is no immigration policy that can fix this on the timescales required, and even sharply rising fertility rates from policy interventions (which Korea has been trying for 20 years with almost no measurable effect) would not produce working-age adults until 2045 at the earliest. Korea's bet is that capital and automation can substitute for the workers it does not have, fast enough to keep the country economically competitive through the demographic trough. Whether that bet works will be one of the most important natural experiments in macroeconomics this century. If it does, every other aging society will copy the playbook. If it doesn't, the conventional pessimism about aging societies will get fully validated in the worst-case form.

## Uncomfortable question

Pick the country you live in (or the country you most identify with) and answer this honestly: what is its total fertility rate, what is the projected dependency ratio in 2050, and what is the country's stated plan for funding the existing pension and healthcare promises under that demographic structure? If you don't know the numbers, the question is whether you have been thinking about your own country's macro future in any serious way at all. If you do know the numbers, the question is whether the stated plan adds up — and if the stated plan is "we'll grow our way out of it via productivity gains," your follow-up question is *which specific technologies, which specific sectors, and what is the realistic productivity growth rate that gets you there*. Most countries' answers, when you actually push on them, don't add up. The not-adding-up is the macro story of the next 30 years. The question in the meantime is what *you* do with that knowledge.
