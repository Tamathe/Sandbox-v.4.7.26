## Reading

There is a quiet fact at the center of the modern AI industry that most observers have not made peace with: **the cost of training a frontier model has gone up roughly 10x every two years for a decade, while the cost of using one has gone down even faster.** Those two numbers are not in tension. They describe the same thing. The training cost is the entry fee for being one of the labs that produces the model. The inference cost is the price the rest of us pay to consume what the labs produced. The first number, the entry fee, has crossed thresholds that exclude almost everyone. By 2026, training a true frontier-scale model costs hundreds of millions to low billions of dollars per run, and you need a multi-gigawatt datacenter and a supply contract for tens of thousands of high-end accelerators that you can't simply buy on the open market. The number of organizations on Earth that can write that check and execute the build is in single digits.

This is what people mean when they talk about **AI scaling laws** and the **compute divide**. The scaling-laws result, established empirically by 2020 and reconfirmed in every generation since, says roughly: model capability grows predictably with the amount of compute and data you throw at it, in a power-law relationship with no clean ceiling that anyone has hit yet. Every order of magnitude more compute buys you a roughly predictable improvement in benchmarks, in coherence, in reasoning depth. The implication is simple and brutal: *whoever can spend more compute will have a better model, and the gap is predictable, not random*. This is the opposite of how most of software has worked. In normal software, a small team with a clever idea can disrupt a giant. In AI, the giant has the compute and the small team does not, and the small team's clever idea, run on the giant's compute, would also produce a better result on the giant's hardware.

The result is that the AI industry is, structurally, not like the early-2000s internet (which was startup-friendly and produced a long tail of small successes). It is more like the semiconductor industry (which has been consolidating since the 1980s and now has effectively three companies that can fabricate leading-edge chips). It is more like the airline industry, the electric utility industry, the petrochemical industry. It is a *capital-intensive* industry with strong returns to scale, and capital-intensive industries with strong returns to scale concentrate. That is not a moral claim, it is an arithmetic one.

Now, none of that would matter much if AI were just one more industry. It matters because AI is becoming an *input to almost every other industry*, the way electricity and software did before it. The thing that concentrates is not just "the AI sector." It is the upstream supplier of the productive capacity of much of the rest of the economy. If three companies own the foundation models that everyone else builds on, those three companies have a structural position the modern economy has not had to grapple with since maybe the early days of Standard Oil. The historical analogues are not all bad — electric utilities concentrated and we did fine — but they all involved heavy regulation, and the regulatory framework for AI in 2026 is, charitably, not yet built.

It's worth distinguishing two kinds of concentration so we can argue about them clearly. **Natural concentration** comes from genuine returns to scale: it really is cheaper per unit to train a 10x bigger model in one big run than to train 10 small models, the same way it's cheaper per kilowatt-hour to run one big nuclear plant than 100 small ones. You cannot legislate this away without throwing away the efficiency. **Policy-driven concentration** comes from regulatory moats, intellectual property regimes, exclusive data access, and political relationships — these *can* be legislated away, and the politics around doing so are exactly the politics that incumbent labs have an interest in shaping. In practice both kinds operate at the same time and it is hard to separate them, but the distinction matters because the policy responses are completely different. Antitrust action against natural concentration is futile and possibly destructive. Antitrust action against policy-driven concentration is potentially very effective. People who don't make the distinction end up advocating for the wrong tool.

What about capital itself, separate from compute? The capital concentration story has been running independently of AI for 40 years — wealth shares for the top 0.1% in OECD countries are at levels not seen since the 1920s, the post-2008 monetary regime of low rates and quantitative easing systematically inflated asset prices held overwhelmingly by the asset-holding class, and the labor share of national income has fallen across most developed economies. Now layer AI on top of that: AI raises the productivity of capital relative to labor (because the marginal cost of capital-substituted labor is collapsing), and the people who own the AI capital are a small subset of the people who already own the capital. The two trends compound. This is one of the few places where the strong "capital concentration is going to get a lot worse" prediction is genuinely defensible from base rates alone, before you get into any particular political analysis.

Now the policy responses. Each gets a fair hearing and a fair criticism, because most discussion of these is tribal and unhelpful.

**1. Antitrust and breakup.** Case for: when an industry concentrates in three players, competitive discipline weakens and rents accumulate. Standard Oil and Bell Labs were both broken up productively. Case against: AI returns to scale are real, breaking up labs that need scale would hand the lead to whichever foreign lab is exempt, and the historical record on tech breakups is mixed. My read: there is a serious antitrust case about *vertical* integration (chip + cloud + model + application) at single firms, and a much weaker one against horizontal scale within any single layer. The antitrust hawks are mostly aiming at the wrong target.

**2. Public compute and public models.** Case for: if compute and frontier models are public infrastructure, treat them as such — government-funded, openly licensed, available to researchers at cost. Case against: governments are bad at running compute clusters, the abuse-prevention problems are formidable, and public-option models tend to lag the frontier by 18-24 months because they cannot match private salaries. My read: partial public compute at the research level (NAIRR-style programs) is worth doing. Trying to compete with frontier labs head-on with public funding probably is not.

**3. UBI and redistribution.** Case for: if AI captures most of the gains and labor can't compete, you need a redistribution mechanism or you get political collapse. Case against: UBI studies are mixed, the political economy of "we are paying you because you have nothing useful to do" is corrosive in ways the models miss, and the funding question (who pays, in what currency, on what fiscal base — see Lesson 1) is unsolved. My read: some redistribution is probably inevitable; whether it takes the form of UBI, expanded wage subsidies, or asset endowments to citizens (sovereign-wealth-style) is the open political question of the next decade. Pretending we can avoid it is wishful thinking.

Tie this back to the rest of the course. The concentration story rhymes uncomfortably with the soft-money story from Module 1. Soft money flows disproportionately to those closest to the spigot (Cantillon effect). AI concentration sends rents disproportionately to those closest to the compute. Both produce the same political outcome: a system in which the people who own capital pull further away from the people who only have labor, and the gap is hard to close from the outside. Whether you think this is a problem worth solving — and if so, with which tools — is one of the genuine fault lines you should figure out where you stand on by the end of this course.

## Concrete example

Between 2020 and 2025, NVIDIA's market capitalization went from about $300 billion to over $3 trillion. Two facts are worth pulling out of that. First, the *physical capital* that NVIDIA produces — high-end accelerators — became the binding constraint on the AI industry. By 2024-25, getting access to a supply contract for the latest generation of NVIDIA chips required either being one of the top-five hyperscalers, being a sovereign nation, or being a startup that had raised eight figures specifically to get in line. Second, the *gross margin* on those chips was around 75% — the kind of margin that economists call a *rent*, indicating limited competitive pressure. Both AMD and a small handful of internal silicon programs at hyperscalers (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium) were trying to break that lock, with limited success. The lesson is not "NVIDIA is evil." It is that an industry growing this fast, with returns to scale this strong, naturally produces one or two firms that capture an enormous share of the value creation, and that this is the kind of structural fact policy has to start with rather than wave away. NVIDIA is the most obvious case; the same dynamic is operating one layer up at the model labs and one layer down at TSMC.

## Uncomfortable question

If you accept that AI scaling laws produce concentration, and if you also accept that concentration of an economy-wide input layer is politically and economically dangerous, then *which specific intervention* do you actually support? Not "we need to do something" — which something, paid for how, applied to whom, with what enforcement mechanism, and what is your honest forecast of what the side effects will be? Most people who are loud about AI concentration cannot answer this question past the slogan stage, which is why the conversation isn't going anywhere. If you can answer it, write your answer down and reread it in six months. You will probably notice you were wrong about something. That is the point.
