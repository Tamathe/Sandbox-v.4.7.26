## Reading

Here is a claim I want you to take seriously, and then attack: **the actual base layer of any economy is not money. It is energy.** Money is a coordination layer that sits on top. Labor and capital and information all run on energy. Take energy away and none of the higher layers do anything. Double the available energy and almost everything that is currently expensive becomes cheap. This is not a metaphor. It is the literal physics of the situation, and it is the single most important fact macroeconomics keeps quietly forgetting.

The reason this matters for our course is that the next ten years are going to be the most energy-hungry decade in modern history, and the technologies this course is about — Bitcoin mining, AI training and inference, electrified transport, datacenter buildout — are all in the same line at the same buffet. Macro that ignores energy is going to be wrong about almost everything that matters between now and 2035.

Let's start with a measure that almost no one outside the energy world uses but that should be part of every educated person's vocabulary: **EROI**, or **energy return on investment**. EROI is the ratio of the energy you get out of a source to the energy you have to put in to extract, refine, and deliver it. Conventional oil from a Saudi or Texan field in 1950 had an EROI somewhere around 100:1 — for every unit of energy you spent drilling, you got 100 units back. That ratio is what built the post-war economy. It is what made personal cars affordable, suburbs viable, global supply chains profitable, and the entire mid-century American standard of living possible. You cannot understand the post-war boom without understanding that the boom was running on, effectively, free energy.

Modern energy sources have lower EROI. Tight oil from US shale runs maybe 5-20:1 depending on the play and the year. Tar sands are around 3-5:1. Solar PV in good locations is around 10-20:1 over the panel's lifetime, which is impressive given how recent the technology is, but the variability is the catch — it doesn't deliver when you need it without storage, and storage shaves the effective number further. Wind is in a similar range. Nuclear, when you actually build it, is in the 50-100:1 range and is the only zero-carbon source with an EROI in the same league as conventional hydrocarbons. Coal varies wildly. The trend across sources is clear: the easy stuff has been used up first, and the marginal new energy is harder to extract than the stuff it is replacing. EROI is, in the long run, drifting down.

Why does this matter? Because **GDP is, in the medium run, almost a linear function of energy consumption.** Not perfectly, not in every quarter, and the relationship has been weakening as economies get more efficient and shift toward services — but the underlying relationship is real and stubborn. Every dollar of GDP requires some quantity of joules to produce, store, and deliver. The ratio of those (energy intensity) has been falling slowly, but the absolute number of joules required to support a 1% rise in GDP keeps going up because the GDP itself is bigger. This is the awkward fact at the bottom of every "we will grow our way out of the debt" story. You can't grow your way out without more energy, and more energy at the scale required is now, in the OECD, a hard problem rather than an easy one.

Now drop the AI and Bitcoin layers on top of this. As of 2026, AI training runs are consuming gigawatt-scale amounts of electricity per cluster. The largest training runs of 2025-26 used as much electricity in a few months as a small country uses in a year. Inference at scale — running the trained models for billions of users — is consuming a comparable or larger total. Bitcoin mining globally is somewhere around 0.5-1% of world electricity, depending on the year and the source. If you add AI and Bitcoin together, you have a meaningful new electricity demand sector that did not exist 20 years ago, growing fast, and competing for the same grid capacity as everyone else. The major US utilities revised their long-range demand forecasts upward in 2024-25 for the first time in a generation, specifically because of AI datacenter demand. That is a real macro-level shift that is going to play out across electricity prices, grid investment, and ultimately monetary policy.

Two important wrinkles. First, **Jevons paradox**: when a resource becomes more efficient to use, total consumption of it usually goes *up*, not down, because the cheaper it is to use the more uses appear. Better LEDs did not reduce lighting electricity consumption — they increased the total amount of lit space. More efficient AI inference will not reduce total AI electricity consumption — it will multiply the number of places AI gets deployed until total electricity demand is even higher. Anyone who tells you "AI is getting more efficient so the energy concern will go away" is making a Jevons error.

Second, **stranded energy**. There are places in the world where energy is produced but cannot be efficiently exported — flared gas at remote oilfields, hydroelectric capacity in the middle of nowhere, solar overproduction at noon in desert grids that have nowhere to put the surplus. This stranded energy is essentially wasted. Both Bitcoin mining and (increasingly) AI compute can run *anywhere there is power*, which means they can monetize stranded energy that has no other buyer. This is not a trivial point — it is one of the few genuinely interesting things to say about Bitcoin's energy footprint that is neither a defense nor an attack. A Bitcoin miner running on flared gas is reducing methane emissions and turning a wasted resource into a useful one. An AI training cluster co-located with a stranded hydro project is doing the same. The question of whether these uses are "good" depends on the counterfactual. If the alternative is the energy being wasted, the answer is one thing. If the alternative is the energy being used for something else, the answer is another. Honest analysis tracks the counterfactual rather than the headline number.

Tie this back to Module 1 and to lesson 1 of this module. Hard money requires honest accounting of what an economy can actually produce. Honest accounting requires honest energy accounting. Energy accounting is currently hidden inside CPI (which weights energy lightly because of substitution assumptions that are wrong on the timescales that matter) and inside the GDP deflator (which has its own problems). The result is that policymakers consistently underweight energy in their models and are then surprised when energy shocks blow up their forecasts. The 1973 oil shock blew up Keynesian models. The 2022 European energy shock blew up the "transitory inflation" model. The next energy shock — and there will be a next energy shock — will blow up something else, and the people writing the models will once again be surprised that the base layer asserted itself. Don't be one of them.

## Concrete example

In 2024, the state of Texas saw its peak summer electricity demand exceed 85 GW for the first time. Roughly 4-5% of that peak was attributable to crypto mining and AI datacenters, both of which had migrated to Texas because of cheap intermittent wind and solar plus a deregulated market that paid loads to curtail during stress. The datacenters became, in effect, *paid demand response*: ERCOT (the Texas grid operator) was sometimes paying them more to *not run* during crisis hours than they would have made running. This is a small but genuinely interesting case of computation acting as a flexible buyer of intermittent renewable energy. It is not a complete answer to anything. But if you had told a 2010 grid engineer that crypto miners would be load-balancing the Texas wind fleet by 2024, they would have called you crazy. The relationship between digital industry and the physical grid is now one of the most consequential and least-understood economic stories in the OECD.

## Uncomfortable question

If you accept the premise that energy is the base layer and that AI and Bitcoin are both going to push electricity demand up materially in the next decade, then the politics of *new energy generation* — nuclear, gas, even coal in some cases — become enormously important to the future you want to live in. Most people in tech are vaguely pro-renewable, vaguely anti-nuclear, and have not actually run the numbers on whether the grid they are planning to plug their AI cluster into can support the demand they are going to create. So: what is the fastest legal path to adding 100 GW of dispatchable, low-carbon electricity to the US grid in the next decade? Be specific. If your answer is "more solar," you have to also answer the storage question, the transmission question, and the land-use question. If your answer is "nuclear," you have to answer the regulatory and labor questions. If your answer is "we'll figure it out," you are dodging the question, and the dodging is itself a political position. Most of the public debate about AI ethics and Bitcoin energy use is downstream of this question and refuses to look at it. You should look at it.
